# Teaching order and tutorial load: explain the robot first, or let the player meet it and explain it the next morning

> Source flags used below. **(search summary)**: taken from a search engine's summary of the page, not from reading the page itself; treat the wording as approximate. **(repo report)**: already verified in `reports/Lessons from acclaimed desk games.md` and only referenced here, not repeated. **(fan wiki)**: fan-sourced. **(design doc)**: facts about this game from `notes/game-design.md`. Everything else was read at the source during this pass (30 Sep 2026).

## 1. One mechanic at a time: Nintendo, Valve, Blow, Teikari, and cognitive load

### Takeaway
The designers behind the most-studied teaching levels agree on the order. Introduce one new element at a time, first in a setting where nothing is at stake, then develop it, then twist it. The learning research adds two limits: people hold only about four new chunks at once, and for simple mechanics players learn more by experimenting than by reading. By that standard, today's day 1 carries more than the benchmark. It has two rules, one of which arrives with its own physics, plus the Inspect tool, where Papers, Please's day 1 has one rule. The proposed alternative turns the robot's first appearance into Nintendo's "introduction" step, with no stakes, but it moves the Rule 0 lesson onto day 2.

### Cited Findings
- Koichi Hayashida (director, Super Mario 3D Land) describes Nintendo's four-panel stage structure: "you introduce a concept, and then in the next panel you develop the idea a little bit more; in the third panel there's something of a change-up, and then in the fourth panel you have your conclusion" — [Game Developer, C. Nutt, 13 Apr 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-secret-to-i-mario-i-level-design)
- Hayashida on the player's side of it: "First, you have to learn how to use that gameplay mechanic, and then the stage will offer you a slightly more complicated scenario in which you have to use it. And then the next step is something crazy happens that makes you think about it in a way you weren't expecting. And then you get to demonstrate, finally, what sort of mastery you've gained over it." He credits Miyamoto's comic-strip background and stresses "a clear concept in the beginning" that is "carried through absolutely all the way" — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-secret-to-i-mario-i-level-design)
- In the structure's name (kishōtenketsu), the four steps are introduction, development, twist and resolution, taken from an East Asian narrative form — [MCV/DEVELOP](https://mcvuk.com/business-news/publishing/video-nintendos-level-design-secrets-in-four-steps/) (search summary; the exact source among the results was not verified)
- Super Mario Bros. World 1-1: in a 2015 Eurogamer interview, Miyamoto and Tezuka say they had to teach several things in one section: avoiding enemies, destroying them, question blocks, and telling a Goomba from a helpful mushroom. The mushroom moves *toward* the player, so a player who has learned "mushroom-shaped things are bad" still meets it and learns it is good. Miyamoto: "We kept simulating what the player would do… So even within that one section, the player would understand the general concept of what Mario is supposed to be" — [GoNintendo summary of the Eurogamer interview](https://www.gonintendo.com/s/100479-miyamoto-explains-how-the-player-is-taught-that-goombas-are-bad-and-mushrooms-are-good); [Eurogamer video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRGRJRUWafY) (search summary)
- Valve on Portal 2 test chambers: "When players first encounter a tractor beam, they're given a very clean room to safely experiment with it." Then: "Once we feel comfortable that players understand the basic rules, we start to enumerate the interesting things that can be done with the mechanic." And: "Players very rarely look up, and they almost never look at the things that are obvious to us as designers… if playtesters miss it time and time again, we know that we need to draw more attention to it" — [Game Informer, 2010](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber.aspx)
- Portal places its first portal so that players see themselves through it (repo report; [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/still-alive-kim-swift-and-erik-wolpaw-talk-portal))
- Jonathan Blow (Braid): every puzzle is meant to bring something new ("no filler"), and he teaches mechanics subtly rather than "having characters explain obvious things repeatedly", an approach he set from the start of Braid — search summary of [Game Developer, "The Path to Braid"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/jonathan-blow-the-path-to-i-braid-i-) and [NYU Game Center](https://gamecenter.nyu.edu/game-designers-in-detail-jonathan-blow/); exact source of each line not verified (search summary)
- The Witness gives no direct instructions. Simple panels sit next to slightly harder ones, each "wordlessly" teaching a new rule, so that players come to their own epiphanies — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Witness_(2016_video_game)) (search summary)
- Baba Is You: Arvi Teikari aimed for every level to "at least try to incorporate some new interaction or feature of the game system", and much of testing went into removing uninteresting alternative solutions — search summary of interviews ([Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-hempuli-oy-s-i-baba-is-you-i-); [Game Pilgrim](https://gamepilgrim.com/2019/04/06/developer-interview-hempuli-oy/)). A fetched Game Developer interview mentions him avoiding levels that overwhelm players with too many active rules, but has no direct quote on tutorials — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-i-baba-is-you-i-s-delightfully-innovative-rule-writing-system)
- Working memory holds about four chunks (three to five), not Miller's seven — [Cowan 2001, Behavioral and Brain Sciences](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496/S0140525X01003922a.pdf/the-magical-number-4-in-short-term-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity.pdf) (search summary)
- Counterweight, Mayer's "pre-training principle": people learn better from a complex explanation when they already know the names and characteristics of its parts. In Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell (2002), learners pre-trained on the parts of a brake system or a tyre pump did better on transfer tests than those who were not (large effects, d ≈ 0.8–0.9 reported) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12240927/); [Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/pretraining-principle/01791D57F5D4164251269E6DF56A8BF1) (search summary)
- Andersen et al. (CHI 2012) found "no evidence to support the claim that restricting player freedom in order to focus the player's attention on a target concept improves learnability" — [Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- Benchmarks already in the repo: Papers, Please's day 1 has one rule, readable at a glance; Pope's day 3, where "the leap… from just stamping to being gated by inspection is too much" (repo report; [Pope devlog, Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)). The Wikipedia page fetched in this pass says only that rules "become progressively more difficult over time" and does not state the day-1 rule, so that detail rests on the earlier report — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- This game today: day 1 has five applicants and no clock. Rule 1 (the phrase) is the new rule. Rule 0 sits on the first page, with the rulebook opened there at every call. The welcome letter explains what a unit is and why it lights up. Day 1 also teaches one guided Inspect, and the first unit is the third applicant. Day 2 adds the photo rule, introduces the clock, and brings a unit that shuts its eyes twice (design doc, `notes/game-design.md`, day table)

### Inferences
- By the one-new-thing standard, today's day 1 teaches at least four new things: stamping a verdict, reading the video stills, Rule 1 with a guided Inspect, and Rule 0 with its lamp physics. Only the first three are needed to play the day. The lamp paragraph is a second lesson on a tutorial day.
- The alternative matches the "clean room" and moving-mushroom pattern: the player meets the new element where nothing can go wrong. Day 1's unit then plays the kishōtenketsu "introduction". Day 2 develops it (the rule, and the unit blinking twice), days 4 and 5 twist it (the papers and the face catch the unit, not the lamp), and day 7 concludes it (the lit slit). The week's lamp thread already has this shape; the alternative only moves the first rule off the introduction step.
- The alternative does not remove load; it moves it. Day 2 already adds the photo rule and the clock. Adding Rule 0 would make day 2 the first day with two new rules plus a new pressure. Pope hit his cliff at exactly that kind of stacking. If the owner takes the alternative, the evidence argues for keeping day 2 to one new rule or one new pressure, for example by adding the lamp check without a second new rule that day, or by letting the clock arrive a day later. These are design choices for the owner.
- Mayer's pre-training result is the strongest case for today's order, but it concerns naming the parts before a causal explanation, not stating a rule before an example. The rulebook's Fig. 0 (the specimen face with and without the light) does pre-training's job in one picture. The physics paragraph goes further than pre-training needs.

### Gaps
- No primary source was found in which Blow or Teikari states a "one new idea per level" rule verbatim. The lines above come from search summaries.
- Game Maker's Toolkit transcripts (e.g., on Super Mario 3D World's four-step design) were not retrieved.
- A search summary says each Outer Wilds "Curiosity" has about three clues, never two on the same planet, which would be a spacing rule. The exact source was not identified, so it is not used above.

## 2. Text-heavy tutorials: do players read welcome letters and bulletins?

### Takeaway
Mostly not. Across HCI and game studies, most people skim or skip instructional text and learn by doing. They benefit from explanation mainly when it arrives at the moment of need, and mainly in unconventional games. A paragraph of robot physics, read before the first applicant, is the weakest form measured: up-front and out of context. The lamp is also the least conventional mechanic in this game, which is where in-context teaching measurably helps most.

### Cited Findings
- Andersen et al. (CHI 2012) ran a multivariate experiment with **over 45,000 players** of three games they built: Refraction, Hello Worlds and Foldit. Tutorials "increased play time by as much as 29% and player progress by as much as 75%" in Foldit, the most complex and least conventional, but had "a surprisingly negligible effect" in the two simpler, genre-typical games — [Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- Just-in-time beats up-front, but only where the game is complex: "Giving tutorial instructions as closely as possible to when they were needed, rather than out of context in an up-front manual, increased play time by 16% and progress by 40% in Foldit but had no effect in the other two games" — [Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- Tutorials can cost players. In Hello Worlds, players given context-sensitive tutorials returned less often than players given none: 17.96% against 21.60% (p < 0.001, Table 3) — [Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- Help on demand is not a free fix. "Only 31% of players that had access to the help button in Refraction ever used it", and the button "reduced player progress by 12% and play time by 15% in Refraction". The authors' conclusion: "the players of these games, at least, seem to learn more from experimentation than from reading text", so designers should "design early levels in a way that maximizes a player's ability to experiment and discover game mechanics" — [Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- In a study of about 50,000 students in an educational game, all four hint systems tested lowered performance compared with no hints — [O'Rourke, Ballweber & Popović, L@S 2014](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2556325.2566248) (abstract via search summary)
- Carroll & Rosson's "paradox of the active user" (IBM, 1987): "Learners at every level of experience try to avoid reading." People "are so busy trying things out… that they often do not notice the small voice of structured instruction crying out to them from behind the manual." Also: "Users become very frustrated when training 'introduces' them to function but expects them to refrain from using it to perform a real task." One learner said: "I want to do something, not learn to do everything" — [Carroll & Rosson](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/4.mental.mental.carroll.paradox.pdf)
- Carroll's task-oriented "Guided Exploration" cards beat a commercial self-study manual. Learners "spent substantially less time yet still performed better on a transfer of learning post-test", and the cards were "nearly 3 times as efficient as the manual" — [Carroll & Rosson](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/4.mental.mental.carroll.paradox.pdf)
- On the web, users have time to read at most 28% of the words on an average page, and 20% is more likely (25 instrumented users, 593-word pages on average) — [Nielsen, NN/g, 2008](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) (search summary)
- In first-hour reviews, "players wait for cut-scenes to end, skip tutorials, skip crafting systems; reviewers count the minutes to action." Also: "If a player misses a key detail in learning about the game and it is a deal-breaker, he will abandon the game… primarily an information problem rather than a game-play issue" — [Cheung, Zimmermann & Nagappan, CHI PLAY 2014](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/cheung-chiplay-2014.pdf) (247 reviews and 6 industry interviews)
- Pope found that "people will probably miss important bulletin info no matter what". His citations naming the specific error became the real tutorial, and he had to add a first-night message because players missed that their family depended on their pay (repo report; [devlog Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/), [Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/))
- Valve moved GLaDOS's lines to the start and end of a chamber because players "really, really don't want to listen to her while they're actively thinking through a puzzle" — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber.aspx)

### Inferences
- Expect most players to skim the welcome letter's robot paragraph whichever teaching order is chosen. Rule 0's reason therefore cannot live only in the letter. It has to reach the player where their eyes already are: on the video still, on Fig. 0-2, on the slip, or in the Gazette's picture.
- Andersen's result gives both sides something. The lamp is an unconventional mechanic, so teaching it helps. But the help that worked was in context, at the moment of need, not in an up-front manual. In the alternative, the desk's reply when the player points at the light ("These disagree, but no rule in force covers it. Yet.") is that in-context moment. The Gazette's explanation the next morning is out of context, so it should reprint the still the player saw.
- The desk line should respond to something the player did (pointing at the light), not sit behind a help button. In Andersen's and O'Rourke's studies, on-demand help went mostly unused and sometimes lowered performance.
- Carroll's "introduced but not allowed to use it" frustration is a real risk for the alternative. The player finds the light but is told no rule lets them act on it. The word "Yet." and a payoff the very next morning are what keep it from reading as a withheld button. Waiting more than a day would weaken the promise.

### Gaps
- No data was found on how many players read in-game letters or bulletins in desk games specifically. Pope's observation is qualitative.
- This game has no telemetry or recorded first sessions on whether the day-1 letter is read. A recorded first session, as the repo report recommends, would answer this directly.
- Frommel et al. (2017) on context-sensitive tutorials in VR was not retrieved.

## 3. Show, then tell: curiosity, a later explanation, and whether a rule stated first reads as arbitrary

### Takeaway
The learning science modestly favours letting people meet a case before being told the principle. Across 53 studies the effect on conceptual learning was moderate (Hedges' g = 0.36), and curiosity and surprise improve memory for the eventual answer. It works only when the learner actually engages with the case and the later explanation builds on what they saw. Designers who rely on unexplained sights (Outer Wilds, Obra Dinn, Papers, Please's bombing) add a warning: an oddity only intrigues against a baseline the player already knows. No study was found showing directly that a rule stated before any example feels arbitrary. The indirect evidence points that way: reasons help people accept rules, and examples before abstraction help understanding.

### Cited Findings
- Loewenstein's information-gap theory (1994, "The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation"): curiosity is a deprivation that arises when attention is drawn to a gap in one's knowledge. A small "priming dose" of information raises it, and gaps that are too large or too small produce little of it — [secondary summary](https://psychologyfanatic.com/information-gap-theory/); see also the review by [Kidd & Hayden 2015, Neuron](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0896627315007679) (search summary; the original paper was not retrieved)
- Kang et al. (2009) found that curiosity about a trivia question predicts activity in reward areas (caudate). People spent scarce tokens or waiting time to learn answers they were curious about, and higher curiosity went with **better recall of surprising answers one to two weeks later** — [Kang et al., Psychological Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/) (abstract via search summary)
- Metcalfe (2017, Annual Review of Psychology): errors followed by corrective feedback improve learning, especially when the person was confident in the error (the "hypercorrection effect"). The benefit depends on the person attending to and processing the correction — [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022) (search summary)
- Schwartz & Bransford ("A Time for Telling", 1998), three classroom studies: students who first analysed contrasting cases and then heard a lecture predicted outcomes a week later "substantially better" than students who read about the features and then heard the lecture, summarised a text and then heard it, or analysed the cases twice with no lecture. Noticing the differences first creates "a time for telling" — [Schwartz & Bransford](https://aaalab.stanford.edu/papers/time_for_telling.pdf) (search summary)
- Sinha & Kapur (2021) meta-analysed 53 studies with 166 comparisons and found a moderate effect in favour of problem solving followed by instruction over instruction followed by problem solving (Hedges' g = 0.36) for conceptual understanding and transfer. The reverse order was favoured for younger learners (grades 2–5) and for domain-general skills — [Review of Educational Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105); [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2021-09-productively-wiser.html) (search summary)
- Loibl, Roll & Rummel (2017): problem solving before instruction helps **only** when specific features are present, namely contrasting cases, or instruction that builds on the learners' own attempts — [Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-016-9379-x) (search summary)
- Fyfe et al. (2014) review "concreteness fading": begin with concrete examples and fade gradually to the abstract rule, rather than starting abstract — [Educational Psychology Review via ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1036777) (search summary)
- Deci et al. (1994): a meaningful rationale for a dull but required task, together with acknowledging that it is dull and stressing choice over control, led to greater internalisation, measured by free-choice time on the task and self-reported attitude — [Journal of Personality, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8169757/) (search summary)
- Explaining the next day rather than at once does not appear costly. Butler et al. (2007) found delayed feedback beat immediate feedback on a test one week later but not one day later. Reviewers note that feedback timing is often confounded with the retention interval, and the question remains debated — [Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2007](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5657616_The_effect_of_type_and_timing_of_feedback_on_learning_from_multiple-choice_tests); [2026 meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-026-10117-8) (search summary)
- Outer Wilds' creative lead, Alex Beachum, on a failed early prototype: "Oh a thing falls from space, that'll get them curious, right? And it just super didn't work at all because players weren't familiar with the village yet… you can't expect someone to be curious about something specific unless they're already familiar with everything in their immediate vicinity" — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/live-die-repeat-how-i-outer-wilds-i-piques-curiosity-in-an-ambivalent-solar-system)
- Pope on Obra Dinn: "My goal for Obra Dinn was to overwhelm the player initially, then give them time to get comfortable with the core loop." Also: "I tried to make each scene interesting on its own and not dependent on a deep understanding of exactly what's going on from the bigger picture" — [Game Developer, Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-lucas-pope-s-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-)
- Golden Idol's designer calls the genre "a matter of trusting the players, which is not often easy". The team tests each case at least 5–7 times, watching "with our mouths shut" as players get stuck — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)
- Papers, Please uses the pattern of a visible event followed by a rule. On day 6 a Kolechian attacker bombs the checkpoint on screen. Day 7's bulletin reads: "Kolechian extremists are suspected in yesterday's bombing. Search all Kolechians before approving their entry." The rule is withdrawn soon after a diplomatic complaint — [Papers Please Wiki, Day 7](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_7) (fan wiki, search summary). More generally, "as relations between Arstotzka and nearby countries deteriorate, new rules are added" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- A first-hour reviewer of Condemned is "intrigued when he discovers that his ammunition is designed to be limited": he finds the rule by doing, then reads it as a "very interesting game concept" — [Cheung et al. 2014](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/cheung-chiplay-2014.pdf)
- Chekhov's gun is the dramatic principle that every element introduced should be necessary to the plot — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chekhovs-gun). Writing-craft sources add that "too little setup can make a payoff feel arbitrary or convenient; too much can make it predictable", and that a setup should register without announcing how it will matter — craft blogs (search summary; e.g., [Literature & Latte](https://www.literatureandlatte.com/blog/chekhovs-gun-examples))
- This game's own precedent and finding: Socrates (born 470 BC) is valid until day 6, and the living rule "is made because of him". The owner found on 29 Sep 2026 that "a light with no reason reads as an arbitrary rule", which is why the lamp's reason moved into the welcome letter (design doc)

### Inferences
- The owner's 29 Sep finding was about a light with **no** reason. Both orders give a reason. The research question is only whether the reason comes before or after the player sees the light, and the evidence (Schwartz & Bransford; productive failure; concreteness fading; curiosity boosting memory for the answer) leans toward after, provided the player noticed the light.
- Schwartz & Bransford's weaker condition, reading about the features and then getting the lecture, is close to today's order: the letter describes the light, then the unit shows it. Their stronger condition, noticing the difference in cases and then being told, is close to the alternative: the still shows the light, the desk says "These disagree", and the Gazette explains. This is an analogy, not a test on games.
- Loibl's condition applies directly. The morning explanation should build on the player's own day-1 case: reprint the still with the lit frame, and name the applicant and the window. "Following yesterday's registration at Window 3 of [the unit's name], a home robot…" works. A general physics paragraph would not.
- Beachum's village lesson applies too. The light only reads as odd if the player has already seen ordinary faces with shut eyes and no light. Today the unit is the third applicant. For the alternative to work, the first two videos must show a person's eyes shut in at least one frame, so there is a normal to break.
- Obra Dinn shows that overwhelming the player at the start can work, but only in a game with no clock and no penalties early. Day 1 here also has no clock, and "nobody can lose on day 1" (design doc), so some unexplained strangeness is tolerable. The risk is confusion about what matters, not failure.
- A later reveal makes the rule feel earned if it answers a question the player had. For a player who never noticed the light, the Gazette is a tell-first explanation again, just a day late. The reprinted still is what saves that case. Valve's "players very rarely look up" warns that the day-1 lamp must be the week's most visible (it already is: "bloom").

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that compares "rule before example" with "example before rule" for a game rule. All ordering evidence comes from classroom and lab learning.
- Nothing found measures whether players judge a rule stated first as "arbitrary". The evidence covers understanding and acceptance, not perceived arbitrariness.
- Her Story and Inscryption were not researched in this pass, and no designer statements on unexplained-first design were gathered for them.

## 4. "Your stamp caused this": the player's correct but unlucky action as the cause of the next rule

### Takeaway
Games routinely let the world answer the player's decisions in the next day's news or laws: Papers, Please's newspaper and bulletins, Headliner: NoviNews, Frostpunk. Players accept this when the consequence follows visibly from something they saw and did. The documented backlash has other causes: being penalised for something the rules never let them act on, and evidence the rules refuse to count. For the alternative, that means no penalty for registering the day-1 unit, a Gazette that blames the Ministry rather than the clerk, and real credit for clerks who spotted the light.

### Cited Findings
- Papers, Please's newspaper pays off specific decisions. Detaining the pimp prints "Human Trafficking Ring Shut Down"; denying him prints "Dancers At Grestin Club Found Dead" (repo report; [Pope devlog, Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/))
- In Papers, Please, "if the player has violated protocol, a citation will be issued via in-game fax… shortly after the entrant leaves" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please). A citation is also issued for denying an entrant whose papers are all correct — [Papers Please Wiki](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Citation) (fan wiki, search summary). Players in Steam threads call it "stupid" to have to approve entrants who seem to be lying — [Steam discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/239030/discussions/0/451850849197885926) (search summary; the thread could not be fetched, HTTP 503, so the wording is unverified)
- The genre's angriest fairness complaints are about reasons "I didn't know were valid" and reference material that disagrees with the desk. Beholder, for instance, refused camera footage of a gun as evidence of the gun (repo report; [Steam reviews](https://steamcommunity.com/app/475550/reviews/))
- Beholder's Ministry issues new directives daily with "increasingly arbitrary laws", from owning blue jeans to crying in public. The arbitrariness is the satire — [GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/beholder-review/); [TheXboxHub](https://www.thexboxhub.com/beholder-complete-edition-review/) (search summary of reviews)
- In Headliner: NoviNews, "every article you publish—or don't—will have consequences on the population's attitude", and on the walk home each night the streets show them (protests, drones) — [Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/918820/Headliner_NoviNews/); [Geeks Under Grace](https://www.geeksundergrace.com/gaming/review-headliner-novinews-pc/) (search summary)
- Frostpunk's Book of Laws starts "by finding solutions to problems that would naturally come up in budding societies" — [PC Gamer interview page](https://www.pcgamer.com/frostpunk-developers-on-hope-misery-and-the-ultimately-terrifying-book-of-laws/) (search summary; the page fetch failed). In the first game, once players "committed to an oppressive decision, there was no turning back"; Frostpunk 2 lets them undo laws. Director Jakub Stokalski: "You only do evil stuff if you're doing it for fun" and "no one wants to be an evil person" — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/frostpunk-2-s-developers-didn-t-want-it-to-be-a-jackass-simulator-)
- Jesper Juul (The Art of Failure) sorts failure along three axes: internal or external (the player's fault or the game's), stable or unstable, and global or specific. Players "feel something like injustice when game designers do not treat us fairly" — [Liz England's review](https://lizengland.com/blog/2016/02/review-the-art-of-failure-by-jesper-juul/); [Juul's book page](https://jesperjuul.net/artoffailure/about.html) (search summary)
- The hypercorrection effect: the more confident someone was in an error, the better they learn the correction, provided they attend to it — [Metcalfe 2017](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022) (search summary)
- This game's own record: on 29 Sep 2026 the design found that a hunch on the day-1 unit was dismissed about 57% of the time, and on about 17% no juror named Rule 0, so "the tutorial day could tell a clerk who suspected the unit that it was registered as human". Juror tiers were changed to fix it. Other relevant design facts: "Nobody can lose on day 1"; the truth is decided "against the registry as it stands", so a fake let in stays registered; Window 7's unit (Nina Penrose) was registered last month "against Rule 0"; and the real policy's first requirement, "a real human", has been in every version since 2021 (design doc)

### Inferences
- The alternative is not a player failure, so Juul's unfair-failure trap is avoidable. The clerk followed the rules in force, and the Gazette should say so: the Ministry had no page for it, the clerk did their job. The joke is the institution learning late, as with Socrates. If the Gazette or the day-2 note implies the clerk should have caught it, the reveal flips from institutional satire to "gotcha", the pattern players punish.
- The most likely "tricked" feeling comes from the clerk who noticed and challenged, and that outcome is the one the 29 Sep hunch fix removed. The evidence (Beholder's refused footage, Papers, Please's complaints about citations for denying valid entrants) says a player told they were wrong while the screen shows they were right is the worst case. Mitigations consistent with the evidence: no fine for a day-1 challenge of the unit. The desk's reply acknowledges the disagreement rather than denying it. The next morning credits the suspicion in the Gazette or the supervisor's note ("Window 3 flagged it; the Ministry had no rule to act on"). Hypercorrection works in the player's favour here: the clerk who registered it confidently learns the lamp most firmly from the reveal.
- The Frostpunk 2 change suggests that a correct-but-unlucky action on a tutorial day should not lock in a lasting cost. The design already handles the analogous case: Window 7's unit is withdrawn in the day-6 Gazette. The day-1 unit could be withdrawn the same morning it is revealed, so it does not linger as a registered voucher the player never chose to trust.
- Real-policy fidelity cuts against "only the phrase is in force on day 1". The real registry has always required "a real human". A version of the alternative that keeps the satire true: Rule 0 is on the book from day 1, but the book has no test for robots yet (no Fig. 0-2). The day-1 unit passes because nobody knew what to look for. Day 2's Gazette announces the lamp check "following yesterday's registration of a home robot". The new thing on day 2 is then a check added to an existing rule, not a new rule. This is an option derived from the evidence, for the owner to weigh.

### Gaps
- No designer statements were found for Reigns or This War of Mine on consequences arriving later or players feeling tricked. The gamepressure interview with 11 bit studios had no direct quotes on blame.
- No study was found that measures "felt tricked" against "felt earned" for delayed reveals in games.
- Primary Papers, Please player threads could not be fetched (HTTP 503).

## 5. How much a tutorial day should carry: rules, cases, guidance, and whether to show the hook

### Takeaway
The benchmark and its closest imitators put one new rule on the first day and one per day after. They script the first cases, guide a single use of a tool, and let the hook appear rather than teaching it. The evidence supports showing the robots on day 1 as intrigue, since the first hour's job is to "fix an anticipated element in the player's mind", but not as a second lesson. The alternative's main cost is where the lesson lands: day 2, which already adds the photo rule and the clock.

### Cited Findings
- Papers, Please: one rule on day 1, a new rule tied to a story beat on later days, and a planned front-loading of "cool stuff to hook the player" (repo report; [Pope devlog, May 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-06/))
- No, I'm Not a Human discloses visitor signs through a morning TV broadcast, with a new sign each day, and real humans can show the same signs, so a sign alone does not prove a visitor — [Fandom wiki, Visitors](https://no-i-am-not-a-human.fandom.com/wiki/Visitors); [Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/all-no-im-not-a-human-visitors-and-how-to-identify-them/) (search summary; the day-1 details could not be verified because the walkthrough fetch returned HTTP 403)
- "Intrigue trumps enjoyment": designers "should ask how a new weapon, enemy, or element might spark the interest of a new player." Existing strategies such as "showing players unaffordable items in a store" aim "to fix an anticipated element in the player's mind and to show them the possible depth of gameplay" — [Cheung et al. 2014](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/cheung-chiplay-2014.pdf)
- The same study: "while skills are being learned, the less emotional route (direction) is preferable. Design the first hour to emphasize direction in a way that both instructs and hints at the depth of the game into the future and save room for discovery after the first hour" — [Cheung et al. 2014](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/cheung-chiplay-2014.pdf)
- Players "count the minutes to action" and "wonder when the game will 'open up' or when they'll finally meet the alien" — [Cheung et al. 2014](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/cheung-chiplay-2014.pdf)
- "I want to do something, not learn to do everything" — a learner in Carroll & Mazur's training study, quoted in [Carroll & Rosson](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/4.mental.mental.carroll.paradox.pdf)
- Tutorials pay off in proportion to how unconventional the game is (Foldit against the two genre-typical games) — [Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- Working memory holds about four chunks — [Cowan 2001](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496/S0140525X01003922a.pdf/the-magical-number-4-in-short-term-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity.pdf) (search summary)
- Pope's day-3 cliff, and the repo report's prediction that this game's equivalent is day 4 (a rule, a tool, a persistent registry and a set piece) — repo report; [Pope devlog, Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)
- This game: day 1 has 5 applicants (3 valid), no clock, two scripted openers (one valid, one "hooman") and one guided Inspect. Day 2 has 7 applicants, the clock, the photo rule, and a unit that blinks twice. Day 7 has no new rule (design doc)

### Inferences
- New items per day under each option (rules, tools and pressures, not applicants):
  - **Today:** day 1 has Rule 1, Inspect, and Rule 0 with its lamp physics. Day 2 has the photo rule and the clock.
  - **Owner's alternative:** day 1 has Rule 1 and Inspect, plus the unit as an unexplained sight. Day 2 has Rule 0 (with the lamp), the photo rule and the clock.
  - **The "check added to Rule 0" variant from §4:** the same as the alternative, except day 2's first item is a check added to an existing rule.

  Either alternative lightens day 1 to one rule and one tool, the benchmark load, but makes day 2 the heaviest early day unless something else moves.
- Including the hook on day 1 is supported, as intrigue (Cheung's "anticipated element", Pope's front-loading). Teaching it on day 1 is what the one-thing-per-day evidence argues against. The alternative shows the hook on day 1 and teaches it on day 2. Today's design both shows and teaches it on day 1.
- Guided against unguided: the evidence supports the current split, with two scripted openers and one guided tool use followed by free play. Andersen found no gain from restricting freedom, and Carroll's learners resisted rote steps. It also supports making the first unit unguided. The player should find the light rather than be pointed to it.
- The number of applicants on a tutorial day is barely addressed by the evidence. Five with no clock sits comfortably inside what the genre does. The binding limit is new concepts per day, not cases.
- Whichever order is chosen, the evidence says to check it by watching fresh players (Valve, Golden Idol, Pope). Two questions a recorded first session answers: did the player notice the day-1 light? Could they say what it meant the morning after?

### Gaps
- The number of entrants on Papers, Please's day 1, and its exact first-day guidance, could not be verified (the fan wiki returned HTTP 402).
- No evidence was found on the ideal number of applicants or cases for a tutorial day in inspection games.
- Day-1 structure for That's Not My Neighbor, Not Tonight and Death and Taxes was not found in this pass.
- No A/B study was found comparing a game's hook on the tutorial day against saving it.
