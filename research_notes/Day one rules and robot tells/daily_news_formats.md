# Daily news formats: delivering consequences, story threads and new rules in few words

Source labels used below: **(fan wiki)** = Fandom or other fan wiki, unverified against the game; **(search summary)** = text from a search-engine summary whose page was not opened; **(earlier report)** = cited in `reports/Lessons from acclaimed desk games.md`, not re-fetched here; **(Steam review)** = one player's review, with its vote count, verdict and playtime. Steam mention rates are my own keyword counts (method under question 2), retrieved 30 Sep 2026. A mention rate measures what players choose to write about, not what they read.

## 1. Papers, Please's newspaper: layout, words and pictures, reactive headlines, reception, and Pope's own words

### Takeaway
*The Truth of Arstotzka* is a full-screen front page shown at the start of each day, before the booth. It has short headlines and subheadings with pictures in a four-column layout, and its body text is drawn but not legible. A few headlines change with the player's earlier choices. Pope calls headlines his quickest narrative tool and kept dialogue to a minimum. He never left a rule in the paper or the bulletin alone, though: bulletins were "basically ignored" when someone talked, so every rule moved into the rulebook and every mistake is named on a citation. Players barely write about the paper. It appears in about 1% of substantive positive Steam reviews, against about 22% each for the family and the endings. The players who do mention it describe it as optional depth, for those who "read the newspaper".

### Cited Findings
**Layout and length**
- Pope, February 2013: "Each day will start with a newspaper page." — [Pope devlog, Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/)
- Pope on the 2022 phone port: "The start-of-day newspaper was another big boy and needed both the 2/3 scale plus a narrower 3-column layout to replace the orignal 4-column layout." [sic] — [Pope mobile devlog, 2022](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/mobile/)
- The paper is published daily. Its front page carries domestic and international news "as well as alternating sections for the weather, entertainment, sports, and politics, but the actual text beyond headlines and subheadings is not legible to the player". It "appears at the start of each day and on the last page of the official bulletin", so it can be read again at the desk. (fan wiki, read through the wiki's API) — [The Truth of Arstotzka](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/The_Truth_of_Arstotzka)
- Headline examples: day 2, "Grestin Checkpoint a Success!"; day 3, "Terrorists Strike At Grestin!"; day 6, "Jobs Few, Becoming Fewer". Unused stories in the game files pair a headline with a subheading, for example "Arson At Ministry Of Agriculture HQ! Crop Output Predictions Severely Affected" and "Cold Fails to Relent, Heating Costs Spiral". (fan wiki) — [Day 2](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_2), [Day 3](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_3), [Day 6](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_6), [The Truth of Arstotzka](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/The_Truth_of_Arstotzka)
- "The layout for each day varies from game to game but the main headlines stay the same." (fan wiki) — [The Truth of Arstotzka](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/The_Truth_of_Arstotzka)

**Headlines that answer the player's choices**
- Pope's own example: detaining the pimp prints "Human Trafficking Ring Shut Down! Grestin Checkpoint Officer Nabs Mastermind"; approving or denying him prints "Dancers At Grestin Club Found Dead! Newly Immigrated Girls Victims Of Human Trafficking". — [Pope devlog, Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)
- In beta, a particular sequence of clicks could both admit and arrest him (Pope described how to reproduce it). A player who did this received "both headlines the following day", which "really messes up the newspaper". — [Pope devlog, May 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-06/)
- "Some headlines change to reflect the choices the inspector has made earlier. For example, there are three different headlines about Vince Lestrade on day 6, depending on whether he was detained, denied, or allowed to enter on day 5." The wiki adds that it is "sometimes best to read these carefully before proceeding to work at the border as the information given can contain vital details". (fan wiki) — [The Truth of Arstotzka](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/The_Truth_of_Arstotzka)
- The paper sets up rules without stating them. On day 3 the paper leads with "Terrorists Strike At Grestin!", the entry ticket becomes compulsory "in reaction to the terrorist attack on day 2", and the guards around the checkpoint go from one to five, staying until day 16. (fan wiki) — [Day 3](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_3). The sibling note quotes that morning's subheadings as "Checkpoint Lockdown. More Guards - More Scrutiny" (fan wiki) — `rule_pacing_and_rulebook.md` in this folder.
- Pope scheduled news as a daily item alongside rules: "Each row represents one day and has the news stories, processing (T=traveler, P=penalty, D=detain), special encounters (bribes, story events), nighttime messages, and expenses." — [Pope devlog, Nov 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-10/)

**Pope on headlines and text**
- "Don't quote me but I have these things, where I really rely on them a lot; newspaper headlines or certain things in my games where they are so quick and easy for me, where I just put one in there and it just slots in so nicely with the way I build narratives." — [GamingBible interview, 23 May 2022](https://www.gamingbible.com/features/lucas-pope-papers-please-return-of-the-obra-dinn-and-no-sequels-20220523)
- "I worked hard to reduce the amount of dialog, keep things ambiguous, leave some things unresolved, and to rely on the player's imagination to fill in the blanks." — [Game Developer, Leigh Alexander, 3 Sep 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- "The intro screens show simple illustrations that set the scene, with minimal text." — [Pope devlog, Mar 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-04/). The endings reuse "the image+text style from the intro"; Pope "felt like the endings didn't need a huge payoff… they're succinct and a little vague". — [Pope devlog, Nov 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-10/)
- The precursor, *The Republia Times* (2012), made the newspaper the whole game: the player "determines which stories are included in each day's issue of the *Times*, and how much prominence each story receives". Rock Paper Shotgun: "Simple and not especially challenging… it's compulsive and sharp regardless." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republia_Times)

**Bulletins, the rulebook and missed rules**
- January 2013: "First bulletin in the game. These are waiting on your desk at the start of each day. Later bulletins will contain mugshots of wanted people." — [Pope devlog, Jan 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-02/)
- February 2013: players "will probably miss important bulletin info no matter what", so a detailed warning report "seems necessary". — [Pope devlog, Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/). March 2013: "For now the game will always tell you exactly what you missed." — [Pope devlog, Mar 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-04/)
- April 2013, on the day 3 roadblock: he added a hint "along with a post-it note on the bulletin", and still "lots of people still miss this". — [Pope devlog, Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)
- "I tried very hard to not introduce new rules through the bulletin on a day when someone (guard, supervisor, investigator) talks to you in the morning. In testing, I found that the bulletin is basically ignored when you've got someone to talk to right away, and that causes unintended confusion." — [Game Developer, 3 Sep 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- November 2013: "I decided to add a ton of pages to the rulebook to centralize what was previously a slew of scattered information." "Important information is still introduced in the bulletin but it also gets a place in the rulebook and disappears from the bulletin on the next day." — [Pope devlog, Nov 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-10/)
- On phones, criminal bulletins go on "a little spot on the back wall where they can be hung up"; players "tap a pin in the bulletin to post them on the wall at the start of each day". — [Pope mobile devlog, 2022](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/mobile/)
- The sibling note finds each rule change announced the same morning in "a short, flat Ministry bulletin, usually 2 to 4 sentences", with the day's first scripted entrant meeting the new rule before anyone breaks it. (fan wiki) — `rule_pacing_and_rulebook.md` in this folder

**What players say**
- The paper is rarely mentioned. In 661 substantive positive English reviews (at least 200 characters), 1.2% mention the newspaper or a headline and 0.5% the bulletin. By comparison, 22.5% mention the family, 22.2% the endings, 16.9% "Glory to Arstotzka", 9.7% the stamp and 4.2% Jorji. None of 106 substantive negative reviews mentions the newspaper. (Steam counts over about 4,170 reviews; method under question 2)
- The most-upvoted reviews that mention it treat reading it as optional depth: "In order to fully understand what this game is trying to get at you have to look around, read the newspaper, and possibly look up some of the history of the berlin wall" — [Steam review, 182 votes, positive, 36 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198164849263/recommended/239030/); "You could be empathetic, you could engage with everything, and read the newspapers, and help to thwart the government. Or you could keep your head down, ignore anybody who talks to you, and just do your job" — [Steam review, 85 votes, positive](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198371930993/recommended/239030/)
- One player spells out the chain from paper to rule: "you will be getting a newspaper with the latest news. You made a mistake and let a terrorist enter the country, an explosion will occur in the capital. Why should I care you may be asking? Well, the government will respond with harder border poli[cies]" — [Steam review, 61 votes, positive](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198040964242/recommended/239030/). Another counts it among the desk objects to arrange: "it is up to you to organize your book, newspapers, bribes, gifts etc." — [Steam review, 55 votes, positive](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197989705237/recommended/239030/)
- Otherwise it is atmosphere: "peeking at the turbulent world through newspapers and events" — [Steam review, positive, 8.9 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198435709608/recommended/239030/); "small little details that you get as time goes on between the nations, and the newspapers to little returning side arcs with characters such as Jorji" — [Steam review, positive, 7.8 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198212562096/recommended/239030/)

### Inferences
- **Papers, Please's paper is headline-sized.** From the examples, a headline runs 3 to 6 words and a subheading 4 to 8, so a front page of three or four stories puts roughly 25 to 50 legible words in front of the player. The rest is pictures and illegible texture. This is my estimate from wiki examples, not a count from the game.
- **The Registry Gazette is two to four times longer.** Counting `src/content/gazette.ts`, a morning prints a headline (7 to 10 words), the report (about 20), the rule notice (21 to 37), the thread item (21 to 54), the small notice (9 to 18) and the countdown: about 90 to 125 legible words, all of it real prose. That sits between Papers, Please's headline page and a text-heavy paper such as Suzerain's (see question 2), and is about the length at which NN/g finds readers get through half a page (question 3).
- **Papers, Please never puts a rule only in the paper.** The paper sets the rule up (a headline and a visible change, such as five guards), the bulletin states it once in 2 to 4 sentences, the rulebook keeps it, and the citation names it when it is missed. The Gazette's Ministry notice combines the setup and the statement in one prose paragraph, inside a page players will scan.
- **What players remember from Papers, Please is people and outcomes**, not print: the family, Jorji, the endings and the national slogan. The paper works as a quick jolt ("that's my doing") and as atmosphere, and it does both in about ten words.
- The Gazette's clockless morning already avoids Pope's worst case (a bulletin read while someone talks). His rule still applies to anything competing for the same moment: the PA's first announcement, which plays as the window opens and the first applicant arrives, and Likeness's letter lying on the paper on day 3.

### Gaps
- No primary source gives the number of stories or pictures per edition. The four-column figure is Pope's; the wiki says layouts vary by run. No screenshot was checked.
- I found no critic review that discusses the newspaper, no Pope statement on whether players read it, and no telemetry.
- The day 3 subheadings come from the sibling note's reading of the fan wiki and were not checked against the game's text files.

## 2. How other games deliver consequences and news, and what players read or skip

### Takeaway
Across about twenty games the pattern is consistent. News is praised when it **reports the player's own act specifically, in the world's voice** (Hitman's paper, Headliner's walk home, Plague Inc's escalating headlines, Three Dog's radio). It is also praised when it **hands the player something to use** (No, I'm Not a Human's daily tell, This War of Mine's radio, Strange Horticulture's letters). It is skipped or resented when it is about strangers or an outside world with no bearing on the job (Not Tonight, Death and Taxes, That's Not My Neighbor, Orwell), when it repeats on replay (No, I'm Not a Human, Reigns, Headliner), when it arrives while the player is busy (Not For Broadcast), or when it stands between the player and the decision (Suzerain). In Steam reviews every news channel is a minor topic, mentioned in 1 to 7% of positive reviews, unless news is the game's core verb. Characters and endings dominate what players write about.

- *Pentiment*: "Because of this tight relationship between the abbey and the town, you have a chance to see in great detail the ramifications of your choices." "People will remember that you're the person who pinned the crime on a certain person. That's the heart of the choice and consequence of our story." — [Josh Sawyer, TheGamer, 27 Jun 2022](https://www.thegamer.com/interview-obsidian-josh-sawyer-pentiment/)
- *Frostpunk*: laws come in sequences; "early laws may not affect the status quo significantly, subsequent laws will be more aggressive in nature and result in significant consequences" (the theme of creeping normality). A reviewer praised "tangible consequences and reactions from citizens", and the team tuned dilemmas to avoid being "too subtle to notice or too exaggerated to become comical". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frostpunk)
- *Unpacking* tells a life almost without words: "Through handling items players are forced to consider each one." The designers call it "empathy through detective work". A diploma first hung over the toilet, then stored under the bed, shows a bad relationship without dialogue. — [Game Developer, Holly Green, 22 Mar 2022](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/gdc2022/gdc-2022-unpacking-a-narrative-through-1-000-household-items)

**Cards, letters and end-of-day reports**
- *Reigns: Her Majesty*: "The storylets this time are written by Leigh Alexander; they're each only a sentence or two long", and still made Emily Short laugh "five or six times during my first 40 minutes". — [Emily Short, 24 Jan 2018](https://emshort.blog/2018/01/24/reigns-her-majesty/). Short does not save repeated text: "When you play a text-based game & after awhile find yourself reluctant to read the incoming 2-liners, then you might suspect something is wrong" — [Steam review, 52 votes, negative](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198042225625/recommended/474750/); "you'll see a lot of the same cards over and over again" — [Steam review, 59 votes, positive](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198068059011/recommended/474750/)
- *Return of the Obra Dinn* closes with a letter whose content depends on the player's work: Jane Bird "only sends the book and the monkey's paw if the inspector determined all available fates. Otherwise, she will report that Evans appreciated the investigator's attempt or regretted trusting the investigator, depending on how many fates were determined." The inspector's insurance report compensates or fines each estate "depending on their conduct". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_the_Obra_Dinn)
- *Strange Horticulture*: clues come on cards at the end of each day or "via letters you receive as part of the day's visitors to the shop" (search summary) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_Horticulture). Players count the letters among the details that build the world: "the map and letters make you feel like you're doing real detective work, not just clicking through dialogue" — [Steam review, positive](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198040920434/recommended/1574580/)
- *That's Not My Neighbor*: the end-of-day results list doppelgangers captured, neighbours killed and doppelgangers let in, with a rank from S to F (search summary of the fan wiki) — [That's Not My Neighbor Wiki](https://thats-not-my-neighbor.fandom.com/wiki/That's_Not_My_Neighbor). A player notes that "the calendar is interactive during the campaigns, you can see which neighbors who you accidentally killed" — [Steam review, negative](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199395556501/recommended/3431040/)
- *Cultist Simulator* puts almost everything on the table as cards, including NPCs, lore, "occult influences" and the Suppression Bureau's hunters who pursue the player. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultist_Simulator)
- *Disco Elysium* delivers much of its information through skills that interject in dialogue ("internal debates with one or more aspects of either mind or body"), in a game of "over 1.2 million words" in its Final Cut. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disco_Elysium)

### Inferences
- **Specific beats general.** Every praised example names the player's own act: the Hitman sketch that looks more like you, the pimp headline, the Headliner street that remembers your article. Every resented one reports a world that goes on without the player: Not Tonight's outside world, Death and Taxes' "abstract formulations", That's Not My Neighbor's paper that does not record the neighbour you killed.
- **Useful beats decorative.** No, I'm Not a Human's TV is read because it hands over the day's tell. It is resented when the tell stops mattering ("useless visitor sign"). This War of Mine's radio and Strange Horticulture's letters are used, not just read. For this game, a rule that decides verdicts will stay useful. The thread items and small notices will not.
- **People carry consequences better than print.** Fate is mentioned more than three times as often as Death and Taxes' phone (20.4% vs 5.7% of positive reviews), and Papers, Please's family and Jorji far more than its paper. Headliner's praise goes to the people and places on the walk, not to the articles.
- **A delivery channel that runs during play loses to play.** Not For Broadcast, the old ticker in this game, and Pope's bulletin all show this. Portal's lesson (GLaDOS speaks at the start and end of a chamber, not mid-puzzle; earlier report) points the same way.
- **Replay kills fixed text faster than anything else.** No, I'm Not a Human's broadcasts, Reigns' cards and Headliner's walk are the replay complaints. This game's "Start day N again" and seeded weeks will show the same Gazette text again, which argues for fewer words, not more.
- **A tallied summary is liked when dressed as news.** Hitman's players liked the statistics precisely because they came as a newspaper "rather than showing a tabled list".

### Gaps
- Kentucky Route Zero (its TV interludes), Inscryption and Cultist Simulator's text load were not researched beyond Wikipedia, which had nothing on news delivery. Disco Elysium was covered only for word count.
- Frostpunk's event pop-ups and citizens' voiced complaints are not documented in the sources I could open. The PC Gamer and TechRaptor developer interviews were blocked (403 or truncated).
- Beholder's directive delivery (mail, journal, TV) rests on fan wiki summaries.
- The Steam counts measure mentions, not reading. No game here publishes how often its news screen is opened or skipped.

## 3. Evidence on reading and skipping in-game text: word counts, "tweet length", placement, telemetry

### Takeaway
The guidance converges: at most two lines on screen at once, about 8 to 10 words a line, one or two sentences (about 120 to 150 characters) per item, and around 100 words for a whole screen. Players read headlines and pictures first. They read most of what they choose to read, but they choose a small part of a page. Delivery matters as much as length: text read while something else demands attention is skipped, and "if you give people at least half an excuse not to read something then they won't". I found no published telemetry on skipped text in games.

### Cited Findings
**Research on reading**
- Web users "have time to read at most 28% of the words during an average visit; 20% is more likely". On pages of 111 words or fewer they can read about half; each extra 100 words adds about 4.4 seconds, so about 18% of added text is read. The data covered 45,237 page views. — [Jakob Nielsen, NN/g, 5 May 2008](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)
- Poynter's EyeTrack07 (600 readers): readers enter a printed page through its largest image, and a majority read the headline before the text. (search summary) — [OJR](https://www.ojr.org/early-lessons-from-poynters-eyetrack07/index.html). Once they choose a story they read much of it: "online readers read 77 percent of what they chose to read. Broadsheet readers read an average of 62 percent." Alternative story forms (lists, Q&A, fact boxes) drew about 15% more attention than narrative text. The source criticises the study for measuring what readers chose, not why. — [Brass Tacks Design](https://www.brasstacksdesign.com/eyetrack07.htm)

**Guidance from game writers and designers**
- Peter Angstadt, from playtesting *Cannon Brawl*: "do not put more than 8-10 words on a line"; "I'd recommend that you don't show more than 2 lines at a time"; "Players quickly ignore walls of text". He pauses play while text shows because "People can only focus on one thing at a time". After the change, a feature nobody had tried ("no one tried flanking shields") was tried by "the majority of people". — [Game Developer, 1 May 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-get-people-to-read-text-in-your-game)
- Zynga's narrative team (GDC Online 2012): a top limit of 150 characters, a norm of 123; "Short and punchy: This is what I need you to do. Go do this thing. Clever turn of phrase."; "two sentences is all you ever need". Players also treated good text as collectible and shared it. — [Game Developer, Frank Cifaldi, 10 Oct 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/social-players-don-t-want-to-read-your-text----or-do-they-)
- Jon Ingold (inkle): "if you give people at least half an excuse not to read something then they won't"; readers often "simply play the options without reading" the text above them. *80 Days* delivers ideas "in short snippets… or 100 words or less". Failbetter meters text "in very small portions" so players "focus on it intently and savor it". — [Game Developer, Jack Yarwood, 7 Jan 2022](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-remove-the-frustration-of-reading-in-games)
- Scott Benson (*Night in the Woods*): "I basically wrote everything in tweet length… You sort of learn how to segment longer thoughts, add pauses and single word tweets for humor and tension." "There's nothing worse that opening up a conversation in-game and it's just like paragraphs of unbroken text on screen." "It feels better to click through a strategically segmented and timed series of bits than get hit with one big slab of dialogue." — [Cartridge Lit, 15 May 2018](https://cartridgelit.com/2018/05/15/interview-scott-benson/)
- Greg Lobanov (*Wandersong*, *Chicory*): "CUT OUT AS MANY WORDS AS POSSIBLE FROM YOUR GAME!" "Every line of text you add increases the time players spend waiting and adds another moment where they have to press A to advance." He aims for the impression that "every bit of text in my games matters". — [Lobanov, 4 Apr 2023](https://greglobanov.substack.com/p/game-musings-watch-your-wordcount)
- Pope: bulletins are "basically ignored when you've got someone to talk to right away"; players "will probably miss important bulletin info no matter what"; he "worked hard to reduce the amount of dialog". — [Game Developer, 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-); [Pope devlog, Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/)
- Valve: "even though people like hearing GLaDOS, they really, really don't want to listen to her while they're actively thinking through a puzzle", so she speaks at the start and end of a chamber. (earlier report) — [Game Informer, 2010](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber.aspx)

**Accessibility guidelines on text**
- Basic tier: "Use simple clear language", "Use simple clear text formatting", "Use an easily readable default font size", "Allow players to progress through text prompts at their own pace", "Ensure no essential information is conveyed by sounds alone". Intermediate: "Highlight important words", "Indicate / allow reminder of current objectives during gameplay", "If using a long overarching narrative, provide summaries of progress". — [Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)

**Players skipping, in their own words**
- "skipping entire text after the 20th day as I was so bored" (Not Tonight) — [Steam review, 37 votes](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198091396513/recommended/733790/); "reluctant to read the incoming 2-liners" (Reigns) — [Steam review, 52 votes](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198042225625/recommended/474750/); "I already started skipping most dialogue near the end of my first playthrough" (No, I'm Not a Human) — [Steam review, 200 votes](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198167882320/recommended/3180070/); "wade through hours of news reports" (Suzerain) — [Steam review, 19 votes](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198045162757/recommended/1207650/)
- Players also resent being unable to skip: "You can't skip text or speed it up. It's really annoying." (That's Not My Neighbor) — [Steam review, 24 votes, negative](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198159181875/recommended/3431040/)

**Telemetry**
- Searches for published telemetry on skipped dialogue, codex or tutorial text found none. The articles found assert that skipping is common without data. — [Fred Z., "Why do players skip?"](https://fredzed.substack.com/p/why-do-players-skip)

### Inferences
- **Budget per morning object: one or two sentences, about 20 words, two lines.** The Zynga, Angstadt, Benson and Short evidence all lands there. A headline plus a one-line caption fits; a 54-word thread paragraph does not.
- **Make the one thing they read the thing that matters.** EyeTrack07 says readers commit to what they pick and read most of it. NN/g says they pick little. The headline or picture is the selector: it has to carry the callback by itself, because the paragraph under it is optional.
- **Never show text while something else asks for attention.** This supports the design's clockless morning, and argues against rule text arriving at the same moment as the PA, the supervisor or an applicant.
- **Say the rule twice in two forms, not five times in one form.** The accessibility guidelines ask for a reminder of the current objective during play. Pope's answer was a bulletin once, then the rulebook for good, plus a citation when missed. The current build states the day 6 rule in the Gazette notice (24 words), on the sticky note ("New: they have to blink. Humanity Day tomorrow. Keep stamping."), on the rulebook page, and nearly again in a PA line ("Applicants are asked to blink where the camera can see, and to be filmed, not generated.") — `src/content/gazette.ts`, `src/content/hall.ts`.
- **Short text still wears out when repeated.** Seeded replays of the same day make every Gazette line a repeat.

### Gaps
- No game telemetry on reading or skipping was found. The quantitative evidence is web and newspaper reading, not games.
- EyeTrack07's entry-point findings come from a search summary. The OJR page was blocked, and the one secondary source I opened is critical of the study.
- Angstadt's and Zynga's figures are practitioners' rules of thumb from their own playtests, not controlled studies.

## 4. Diegetic, glanceable alternatives: which carry a callback and a new rule, and which feel original

### Takeaway
The formats with the best evidence for **callbacks** are the ones that need no reading: the consequence walking back in as a person, an object or letter from someone the player stamped, and a photograph that changes because of what the player did. The best format for a **new rule** is the reference the player already uses at the desk: Pope's rulebook, with a short pointer such as a post-it note. Tickers, phone feeds and screens that talk during play are the weakest. Those that are both glanceable and not borrowed from Papers, Please come from the registry itself: a wall of registered faces, the morning post from people the clerk stamped, the rulebook page amended with its cause, a hall board, and a lost-property tray.

### Cited Findings
**Diegetic interfaces**
- Removing non-diegetic HUD elements from a first-person shooter increased immersion **in expert players** by raising "cognitive involvement and sense of control"; the authors stress that expertise changes how interface choices land. — [Iacovides, Cox, Kennedy, Cairns and Jennett, CHI PLAY 2015](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2793107.2793120) (abstract, via [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1470398/); the full text could not be retrieved)

**A picture that carries the consequence**
- Hitman's front page changes its photo with the player's play: a police sketch that "become[s] more accurate as the character's notoriety grows", or the target's photo if the player went unseen. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitman:_Blood_Money)
- In *We Become What We Behold* (Nicky Case, 2016), the player is a news photographer: "Whatever you choose to capture becomes the headline (shown on the TV screen) which then influences what people do". (search summary) — [itch.io](https://ncase.itch.io/wbwwb); [Kill Screen](https://www.killscreen.com/nicky-cases-newest-game-looks-media-shapes-us/)
- Print readers enter through the largest image (EyeTrack07; search summary above).

**Letters and objects from characters**
- Obra Dinn's closing letter varies with how many fates the player solved (see question 2).
- Papers, Please entrants hand the inspector objects that stay on the desk and matter: on day 6 a woman gives a Pink Vice ad and then a note about Dari Ludum, and the player can detain him "by highlighting the aforementioned note and his name on his passport". (fan wiki) — [Day 6](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_6)
- Unpacking's diploma, moved from the wall to under the bed, tells a relationship without a word (see question 2).
- This game already uses a letter as a choice: Likeness's offer lies on the Gazette on day 3 and can be signed or handed in. — `notes/game-design.md`, "The offer"

**The consequence walking back in**
- Headliner's walk home is its most-praised feature, and its most-criticised on replays (see question 2).
- Papers, Please players mention Jorji, who comes back failing each newer rule, three and a half times as often as the newspaper (4.2% vs 1.2% of substantive positive reviews).
- A That's Not My Neighbor player asks for this directly: the neighbour reported missing "should show up the next day". — [Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198184532425/recommended/3431040/)
- Pope's principle: "Don't tell or show something when you can make the player do it themselves." (earlier report) — [Reason, 2013](https://reason.com/2013/09/26/papers-please-politics-in-games-and-the/)

**Visible change in the room**
- Papers, Please's guards go from one to five the morning after the attack. (fan wiki) — [Day 3](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_3)
- This game's Window 2 sign already reads VOTING on days 3 and 4 and FORKED from day 5. — `notes/game-design.md`, "The fork"
- This game's hall already carries rule slogans on posters, two a day in the pixel font, such as "CHECK EVERY FRAME" and "ONE FACE PER HUMAN"; CHECK EVERY FRAME goes back up on day 4, the morning the Gazette reports the dimmed lamp. — `src/content/hall.ts` (`POSTERS`)
- Portal marks each test chamber's entrance with a sign of pictograms for the hazards and devices inside. (fan wiki) — [Portal Wiki, chamber info icons](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Category:Chamber_info_icons)

**Scoreboards and tallies**
- That's Not My Neighbor's results screen tallies captures, deaths and admissions with a rank (search summary). One player finds the end-of-day report "unclear" — [Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198184532425/recommended/3431040/)
- Death and Taxes' world meters come late: "When the indicators appear half the game has passed" — [Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199178361068/recommended/1166290/)
- Hitman's tallies were liked as a newspaper "rather than showing a tabled list" (question 2).

**Phones, TV and radio**
- Phone feeds are the most-criticised channel in this survey (Death and Taxes, Not Tonight; question 2).
- TV works when it hands over the day's tool (No, I'm Not a Human) and fails when it talks during work (Not For Broadcast; this game's ticker).
- "Ensure no essential information is conveyed by sounds alone" (basic tier), so a PA or radio can never be the only carrier of a rule. — [Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)

### Inferences
**How each format carries the three payloads.** Ratings are mine, from the evidence above: ●●● strong, ●● fair, ● weak, – none.

**A shortlist that fits a registry-clerk satire** (proposals, not findings):
1. **The morning post.** One or two envelopes on the desk from people the clerk stamped yesterday, each one line in the sender's own sincere voice. Examples: a thank-you card from someone registered; a complaint from a human the clerk challenged ("I have been human since 1974"); a warranty card from Likeness Robotics "for your new household member", addressed to the registry; a jury summons. It carries the callback through a person, which the Fate and Jorji numbers favour. It extends a format the game already has (Likeness's letter), and filing or binning each envelope would give the morning a verb and a sound.
2. **The registry wall.** Yesterday's registrations pinned as small official photos, each with a REGISTERED, CHALLENGED or REMOVED stamp. A unit the clerk let in hangs among the humans; on day 6 Nina Penrose's photo comes down, stamped WITHDRAWN. The wall has no prose and grows through the week into a record of the clerk's work, and the Humanity Day special can read it back. It is native to the satire: it is what a registry's public list is. My belief that the real Proof of Humanity front end shows a grid of profile photos with status badges is not verified here.
3. **The amended rule page.** The new rule arrives as a pasted amendment slip on the rulebook page, open on the desk: the rule in one line, and under it one line of cause, "Amended following the registration of a man born in 470 BC." The sticky note shrinks to a pointer ("Rule 6 is new."). The rule then lives where it is used, as Pope did it, and its policy-version framing suits a registry whose real policies have versions.
4. **The consequence in the queue.** Keep and widen what the design already does with Pat and the Binnses: a human the clerk challenged comes back with the court's letter saying they are human, or a registered unit's owner arrives to register "the other one". It costs nothing to read, and the evidence-only invariant stays intact because the returning person is judged like anyone.
5. **A one-photo Gazette, if the paper stays.** Masthead, one photograph (the hall camera's still, or the registered unit's official photo stamped REGISTERED), one headline, one caption line, with the rest greeked as in Papers, Please. The rule notice, report, thread and small notice move to formats 1 to 4 and the hall board. This is the least original option and the cheapest.
- **Which carry both a callback and a rule?** Only the queue does both without words: the first applicant after a new rule meets it, and returning people are callbacks. The rest of the evidence favours splitting the jobs: one object for the callback (a letter, the wall or a photo), one place for the rule (the amended page plus the pointer note), and the room for the running story (Window 2's sign, a hall board).

### Gaps
- No study compares these formats directly. The ratings are inferences from single-game evidence.
- The Iacovides result concerns HUDs in a shooter and experts. Its transfer to a desk game's morning screen is untested.
- *We Become What We Behold* and the Portal signs rest on summaries or fan wikis.
- Whether the real Proof of Humanity interface shows a photo grid with status badges was not checked.

## 5. Presenting a new rule each morning so it is noticed, and setting it up in one line

### Takeaway
Rules are noticed when they arrive alone, at the start of the day, in the place the player will use them, and are then tested at once. Pope introduced rules only on mornings when nobody talked, kept each in the rulebook for good, removed it from the bulletin the next day, and named it on every citation. No, I'm Not a Human puts the day's tell at the end of the morning broadcast. Beholder and Frostpunk make the escalation itself the joke. The best one-line setup is a callback to something the player saw: "Following [yesterday's event], [rule]." This game's notices already use that pattern, but at 21 to 37 words, inside a page with four other items.

### Cited Findings
- Pope avoided introducing a rule through the bulletin "on a day when someone (guard, supervisor, investigator) talks to you in the morning", because the bulletin "is basically ignored when you've got someone to talk to right away". — [Game Developer, 3 Sep 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- Rules moved into the rulebook: "Important information is still introduced in the bulletin but it also gets a place in the rulebook and disappears from the bulletin on the next day." — [Pope devlog, Nov 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-10/)
- Citations are the backstop: players "will probably miss important bulletin info no matter what", and "the game will always tell you exactly what you missed". — [Pope devlog, Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/); [Mar 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-04/)
- A post-it note on the bulletin was Pope's hint for the day 3 roadblock, and it did not reach everyone. — [Pope devlog, Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)
- In Papers, Please the cause is sometimes explicit in the bulletin (day 7: "Kolechian extremists are suspected in yesterday's bombing") and sometimes left to the same morning's paper and scenery (day 3's attack headline and five guards). (fan wiki, via the sibling note) — `rule_pacing_and_rulebook.md`; [Day 3](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_3)

### Inferences
- **Put the rule where it is used, and nowhere that talks.** The rulebook page, open on the desk with the new rule marked, is the strongest carrier. The PA and any speaking character should say something else that morning, following Pope's no-talk-on-rule-days rule.
- **Split the notice into the event and the rule.** The event becomes the callback (headline, letter or photo). The rule becomes one line on the page, with the event reduced to a short footnote: "Rule 6. Applicants must be living: born 1900 or later, and seen to blink. (Amended following the registration of a man born in 470 BC.)" Of those words, about twelve are rule, and the cause is optional reading.
- **The best setup line points at someone the player met.** Socrates reaching the window before day 6 is the ideal setup. The rule then lands as the consequence of a stamp, which also serves the "every stamp comes back" pillar.
- **Test it at once.** The first applicant after a new rule meets it (already in the design). Pope and No, I'm Not a Human both make the new rule usable within minutes, which is what keeps it from being "flavour text".
- **Escalation itself can carry the joke.** Beholder's and Frostpunk's players quote the progression, not the wording. A deadpan Ministry changelog read across the week (one amendment slip a day, all visible on the page by day 6) would let the jokes build without extra words in any one morning.
- **One reminder in play, and the citation.** The sticky note on the desk is the reminder of the current objective, and its current wording ("New: they have to blink.") is already at the length the evidence recommends; the Gazette notice repeats it at several times the length. The hall posters are a second, zero-effort reminder in Portal's manner. The citation slip naming the rule is Pope's backstop and already exists.

### Gaps
- No source measures how well rules are noticed in one channel against another. Pope's finding is from his own playtests, reported without numbers.
