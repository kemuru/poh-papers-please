# Fair "is this a human?" checks in games: lessons for Rule 0 in Proof of Humanity: Papers, Please

Scope note: sources checked Sept 2026. Tags: [primary] = developer's own words; [critic] = named publication; [wiki] = fan wiki, TV Tropes or Wikipedia summary without a primary citation I could open; [forum] = Steam or itch.io community posts; [guide] = strategy-guide sites. Several fan wikis (Fandom) and TV Tropes returned HTTP 402/403, so some wiki claims come from search-result snippets. Those are marked "(snippet)".

## 1. That's Not My Neighbor (TNMN): tells, fairness, false positives, escalation, reception

### Takeaway
TNMN mixes checkable document tells (ID number, expiry date, logo, apartment number, the resident folders, a phone call home) with tiny appearance tells (eye spacing, hat creases). Players accept the document and phone checks as fair, but the small appearance differences cause the main complaint: "there is no difference between the doppelganger and the neighbor." Letting in a doppelganger ends the round at once. Killing a real neighbor only lowers your rank, so the game punishes being fooled far harder than being paranoid.

### Cited Findings
- Setup: 1955, the player is a doorman for the Doppelganger Detection Department (D.D.D.) and decides who enters the building. The developer says you "can't overlook any detail because you could be food for doppelgangers." There is a setting to hide neighbors with "holes" (gore) for accessibility. The 1.0 version is free on itch.io; the 2.0 remake is on Steam. [primary] — [itch.io store page, Nacho Sama](https://nachogames.itch.io/thats-not-my-neighbor)
- The doorman's five tools: (1) photo ID, where you check the photo, ID number, expiry date, D.D.D. logo and name spelling; (2) the Entry Request form, with apartment number, reason, name, photo and D.D.D. logo; (3) three resident folders that are "always correct"; (4) the daily visitor list; (5) a rotary phone to call the apartment or the D.D.D. (3312). [guide] — [TheGamer, Eva Csaki, 24 Apr 2024](https://www.thegamer.com/thats-not-my-neighbor-how-to-play/)
- Tell categories: visual defects ("extra body parts, missing body parts, the wrong hair, or the inability to speak"), document problems (missing or wrong info, photos that differ between documents, "suspicious grammar"), and small appearance differences (eye colour, hairstyle, nose shape). [guide] — [TheGamer, 2024](https://www.thegamer.com/thats-not-my-neighbor-how-to-play/)
- The phone as hard evidence: some doppelgangers try to enter while the real resident is already home. "It takes the doorman only one quick call to the apartment" to catch them. [guide] (snippet) — [TheGamer search snippet](https://www.thegamer.com/thats-not-my-neighbor-how-to-play/). In the forum, players list the call as a reliable test: "doppelgangers cannot answer phones." [forum] — [Steam thread "Too identical?", 12 Mar 2025](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3431040/discussions/0/550107357479186440/)
- Uneven penalties: killing an innocent neighbor lowers your rank and the game goes on. Admitting a doppelganger gets the guard eaten and ends the round. Performance is ranked S to F. [guide] — [TheGamer, 2024](https://www.thegamer.com/thats-not-my-neighbor-how-to-play/) (describes the 1.0 version; the 2.0 rules may differ)
- Look-alike innocents: some characters (e.g. Chester) are neither neighbors nor doppelgangers, and some real residents have "convincing excuses" for looking different, which the player must judge. [guide] — [TheGamer, 2024](https://www.thegamer.com/thats-not-my-neighbor-how-to-play/)
- The main fairness complaint: "I've had so many situations now where there is no difference between the doppelganger and the neighbor." Replies said the difference was "the distance of the eyes from the side of his head," eyebrow shape, forehead lines or hat creases, and that "sometimes the DDD logo or the Approval stamp is the difference." One player said later doppelgangers become "nearly perfect." No developer reply. [forum] — [Steam, "Too identical?", 12 Mar 2025](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3431040/discussions/0/550107357479186440/)
- Similar complaints appear in reviews: players looked a doppelganger over, found nothing wrong, and were told at the end that the appearance was different. [forum] (snippet) — [Steam reviews page](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3431040/reviews/)
- Escalation criticism: one reviewer called the game tedious because "every day [is] the same, unlike similar games where each day introduces more conditions, requirements and criterions." [forum] (snippet) — [Steam reviews page](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3431040/reviews/). A commenter on the developer's roadmap post, about Aug 2026: the game lacks "real difficulty spikes in later days" and "difficulty shouldn't come in miniscule details about appearance." [forum] — [itch.io devlog comments, "Roadmap and brainstorming"](https://nachogames.itch.io/thats-not-my-neighbor/devlog/1623751/roadmap-and-brainstorming-for-thats-not-my-neighbor-future)
- Developer's planned changes (roadmap, about Aug 2026): "each neighbor will have a distinct personality, which opens up the possibility for doppelgängers with the wrong personality traits"; "The mechanic of refusing entry is underutilized, so I'm going to give it a better purpose"; and a campaign with "progression in both difficulty and events" and about 10 endings. He also said the 2.0 remake "seemed to displease a lot of people." [primary] — [itch.io devlog, Nacho Sama](https://nachogames.itch.io/thats-not-my-neighbor/devlog/1623751/roadmap-and-brainstorming-for-thats-not-my-neighbor-future)
- A critic's framing: the resident details are randomized each day, so you "can never simply memorize patterns." The review calls it "not a game about power, but about the fear of being wrong." [critic] (snippet) — [Game-Rezone review "Scheduled Paranoia"](https://game-rezone.com/en/review/thats-not-my-neighbor-review-scheduled-paranoia/) (the page returned 404 when fetched)

### Inferences
- Players trust TNMN's reference-based checks: the always-correct folders, the logo and stamp, the phone call home. Players resent the pixel-level appearance diffs, even though these are technically "evidence." This supports the PoH rule that every clue must be clear and checkable against something on screen, never a subtle look.
- The phone call is the strongest pattern for Rule 0. The player asks an independent source a yes/no question and gets an answer that can't be argued with. In PoH, the registry lookup and the video frame can do this job.
- TNMN keeps false positives cheap (rank loss) and false negatives deadly, which suits horror. PoH's economy should punish challenging a valid absurd applicant (e.g. Dave) enough that "challenge everything weird" loses. Otherwise the look-alikes stop mattering.
- The "every day is the same" complaint is the reverse of Papers, Please's daily rule growth. PoH's one-new-rule-a-day structure already avoids it, but Rule 0 must not become a constant that makes the other rules irrelevant.

### Gaps
- No developer interview explaining how tells are chosen or balanced. The devlogs cover business issues and the roadmap, not tell design. The 2.0 remake changelog ("Remake (2.0 version) here", Apr 2025) was not fetched.
- The fan wiki's Doppelgangers page (full list of error types by day) returned HTTP 402, so I could not confirm which tells appear on which days.
- No major-outlet review (RPS, PC Gamer, Eurogamer) found. Reception here is from forums and small sites.

## 2. Papers, Please: discrepancy design, scanner, Jorji, rules-are-rules, fairness and escalation

### Takeaway
Lucas Pope built every error as a correlation: two pieces of the same information that don't match. You flag one by highlighting both in inspect mode, and invasive checks (the body scanner) only unlock after you highlight a checkable discrepancy. Rules grow from one ("Arstotzkans only" on Day 1). Pope spaced new rules so they never compete with story beats and made the warning slip name the exact mistake. Jorji is the model for a gag character who is "one rule behind." Per the wiki he finally arrives with correct papers on Day 11, which is a payoff PoH's Gary can't copy if he is never human.

### Cited Findings
- Core principle, in Pope's words (TIGSource devlog, 3 Feb 2013): "The inspect mode should let you highlight any two pieces of information… The fact that all errors are due to correlation (2 pieces of the same info don't match) gives a clear structure that I think is important." And: "One of my goals is to have all the logical correlations work for any given error." [primary] — [dukope.com devlog archive, TIGSource Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/)
- Feedback that teaches: "In retrospect, people will probably miss important bulletin info no matter what so a detailed warning report seems necessary." And: "Next build will list the mistake on the warning." [primary] — [TIGSource Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/)
- Worry about escalation: "The difficulty will scale up beyond the first few days but I'm definitely worried about getting that scaling right… Maybe a difficulty setting is best." [primary] — [TIGSource Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/)
- Pacing rule introductions: "I tried very hard to not introduce new rules through the bulletin on a day when someone (guard, supervisor, investigator) talks to you in the morning." And: "The bureaucratic structure… made it easy for me to fill out a complex set of gameplay-oriented rules and regulations." [primary] — [Game Developer, Leigh Alexander, "Designing the bleak genius of Papers, Please", 3 Sep 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- Pope on ambiguity: "I also made a concerted effort to keep things vague and non-judgemental throughout the game." [primary] — [Game Developer, 3 Sep 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- Scripted vs procedural mix: "The general flow of the game from day to day, along with 2 or 3 immigrants per day are all scripted. Most of the immigrants are procedurally generated based on the current day's rules." The hard part was "arranging everything to carry the game through 30 days without too many dry spots or unnecessary confusion." [primary] — [Game Developer, "Road to the IGF", Feb 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-lucas-pope-s-i-papers-please-i-)
- Day 1 rule: only Arstotzkan citizens may enter. Rules then add nationality bans and document requirements. Mistakes arrive as fax citations that cost pay. [wiki] — [Wikipedia, Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- Scanner gating: every entrant's weight shows on a booth scale, and you can highlight it against the weight on their papers. After a Day 6 attack a scanner is installed. "The search option will only appear after using inspection mode to highlight one of the two types of discrepancy": a weight mismatch (contraband: smugglers are "a few kilograms heavier") or a sex/appearance mismatch. The scan prints a two-sided photo. [wiki] (snippet) — [Papers Please Wiki: Search / Contraband](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Search)
- Jorji's arc: Day 3, no passport ("Arstotzka so great, passport not required"), leaves when questioned. Day 4, a crude fake passport from the invented country "Cobrastan" (city "Bestburg"). Day 6, a valid passport but no entry permit. Day 8, still no permit, and he presents an entry ticket, which was only valid on Day 3. Day 11, "all of the correct documents with no errors or discrepancies." Day 29, he describes Obristan as a "good place" and tells the inspector how to escape there. [wiki] (snippet) — [Papers Please Wiki: Jorji Costava](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Jorji_Costava), [Cobrastan](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Cobrastan)
- Jorji's character: "very affable, polite, and charismatic despite his criminal lifestyle. He acknowledges that what he does is wrong and commends people who try and stop him"; "everyone's favorite entrant." Also described as a drug smuggler who tries to bribe and charm his way through. [wiki] (snippet) — [Papers Please Wiki: Jorji Costava](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Jorji_Costava). PC Gamer ran a "Great moments in PC gaming" piece on "Dealing with Jorji Costava" (article body not retrievable). [critic] — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/great-moments-in-pc-gaming-dealing-with-jorji-costava-in-papers-please/)
- Pope later made an appearance-first variant himself. He described Mars After Midnight as "Papers Please-lite, with no border checkpoint, no desk, no paperwork, on Mars." The doorman checks aliens through a crank-operated peephole against the night's invitation, using audio and visual clues. Martians are random combinations of facial parts. Critics praised its "inventive silliness." Siliconera found long sessions repetitive. [wiki quoting Pope and critics] — [Wikipedia, Mars After Midnight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_After_Midnight)

### Inferences
- Pope's "every error is a correlation between two on-screen facts" is the same thing as PoH's "one clue tied to one active rule." For Rule 0 it means non-humanity must show up as a mismatch between two on-screen items (e.g. the registered face vs the video frame, or the vouch record vs the signature). It must never be a verdict read from how someone looks at the window.
- The scanner teaches escalation discipline: the invasive or decisive check exists, but you can only use it after you've pointed to a documented discrepancy. PoH could present Rule 0 the same way. It has no standalone "is it human?" test. It is enforced through the evidence each daily rule produces (video, photo match, signature, vouch, duplicate face, blink).
- Warning slips that name the specific mistake are how Papers, Please makes a growing rulebook learnable. When the player misses a non-human, the PoH penalty should name Rule 0 and point at the exact clue.
- The Jorji mapping: each visit fails the rule that is current for him. His Day 8 entry ticket was valid only on Day 3, a literal "one rule behind" joke. His arc ends with legitimate entry and a later favour to the player. If Rule 0 means Gary can never be valid, PoH loses that "he finally made it" payoff. It needs a replacement, such as a day where Gary passes every document check and fails only Rule 0, or a finale where the joke flips.
- A possible clash with PoH's invariant: if Gary always breaks Rule 0 and also breaks that day's newest rule, he has two violations. Papers, Please lets entrants have several discrepancies (the devlog mentions multiple-discrepancy cases). PoH says "one clue." The team should decide whether Gary's single clue is always the humanity clue, shown through the newest rule's evidence, or whether Rule 0 is satisfied by default and the daily rule carries the tell.

### Gaps
- I found no primary Pope quote about Jorji, about "rules are rules" as a phrase, or about fairness in general. Pope's GDC talks were not located or checked.
- The Jorji day-by-day arc and scanner details are from the fan wiki (search snippets; Fandom returned 402). Whether Jorji smuggles drugs on a specific day could not be verified.
- The Wikipedia summary called some critics' view of the paperwork "tedious" but gave no named critic in what I retrieved.

## 3. Other "human or not" games: evidence-based vs appearance-based tells and what that does to fairness

### Takeaway
There's a clear split. Games whose verdict comes from an instrument or a lookup (Blade Runner's Voight-Kampff readout, Papers, Please's scanner, Among Us's visual Medbay scan, TNMN's phone call) read as fair even when the setting is paranoid. Games that rely on bodily "signs" (No, I'm Not a Human; Quarantine Zone: The Last Check) deliberately let innocents show signs, or hide symptoms. Their forums fill with "signs don't matter" and "I missed a symptom I couldn't see" complaints. That ambiguity is a stated design goal in horror and the wrong choice for a fairness-invariant comedy.

### Cited Findings
- **Blade Runner (Westwood, 1997):** The Voight-Kampff test shows a close-up of the subject's eye with three needles: one for human likelihood, one for replicant likelihood, and a sweeping needle for question intensity and pressure. The player picks low, medium or high intensity questions. Too many high-intensity questions end the test with no result. [wiki] — [Wikipedia, Blade Runner (1997 video game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner_(1997_video_game))
- Blade Runner randomizes who is a replicant: of 15 suspects only Clovis and Zuben are always replicants; the other 13 vary per playthrough and their behaviour adapts. Louis Castle: "those agents are real, goal-oriented artificial intelligences… We've given them behavior, we haven't given them instructions." QA reportedly ran the game about 2,500 times to cover the variations. Wrong calls feed into 13 endings rather than a fail state. [wiki quoting Castle] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner_(1997_video_game)); the randomization is also covered in [PC Gamer, "Revisiting Westwood's Blade Runner"](https://www.pcgamer.com/returning-to-westwoods-blade-runner/) (not fetched)
- **No, I'm Not a Human (Trioskaz):** "A new physical trait that can be used to identify Visitors is revealed each day through a television broadcast" (e.g. "flawless white teeth, dirt under the fingernails, and bloodshot eyes"). "Visitors may not exhibit every trait, humans may exhibit some of the traits, and house guests do not always consent to being examined." Most characters are randomized between human and Visitor each playthrough, with a few fixed. Wikipedia calls this "a deliberate design choice by the developers intended to invoke a sense of fear in the player." [wiki] — [Wikipedia, No, I'm not a Human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No,_I%27m_not_a_Human)
- Innocents who show signs (No, I'm Not a Human): the Grave Digger has dirty nails from digging, someone may have red eyes from crying, and the Burned Firefighter shows several symptoms from injuries alone. The guide advises: "aim to corroborate your suspicions with more than one sign." It also says you "can't test for certain things until your character learns about them." [guide] — [TheGamer, Meg Pelliccio, updated 30 Jun 2026](https://www.thegamer.com/no-im-not-a-human-guests-visitor-human-symptoms-signs/)
- Forum reaction: a thread titled "Signs just don't matter at all?" reports "Visitors who passed every single test available, and were still Visitors when killed." Replies split. One says "I can spot visitors with a 100% guarantee"; others say "Some signs could mean visitor to a specific character but human to another," and some characters are "50/50." One user cites a video where a cab driver "had visitor signs while human and all human signs while visitors." No developer reply. [forum] — [Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3180070/discussions/0/597412189643635876/)
- Critics still praised the game's moral pull. Eurogamer's Matt Wales said it encourages players "to find connection and compassion when all hope seems lost." [critic, via wiki] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No,_I%27m_not_a_Human)
- **Quarantine Zone: The Last Check:** a checkpoint with tools (thermometer, stethoscope, a scanner "to look for hidden symptoms under clothing," X-ray). Each symptom is "detected using a tool or your eyes." [guide] (snippet) — [TheGamer symptoms guide](https://www.thegamer.com/quarantine-zone-the-last-check-all-symptoms-sick-infected-detect-guide/). Reviews were "mixed or average" on Metacritic, 41% of critics recommended it on OpenCritic, and it sold over 500,000 copies in its first week. [wiki] (snippet) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarantine_Zone:_The_Last_Check)
- Quarantine Zone forum: survivors "appeared completely healthy but were flagged as infected" at day's end. One player's "perfect 20-day run" was ruined when next-day stats showed "almost every single infected symptom"; another would "ask for a refund." A counter-reply says symptoms hide in "sneaky spots like shoed foot, lower side of hand, inner arms/legs." No developer reply. [forum] — [Steam thread "Missing symptoms??", Jan 27–28](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3419520/discussions/0/726902731209445471/)
- **Among Us:** Submit Scan in MedBay shows a hologram "which can be seen by other players and demonstrates the innocence of the scanned crewmate." Since impostors can't do tasks, visual tasks confirm identity. In v2020.8.31 hosts got an option to turn off visual task animations. Even then, other players must wait for the scanner to clear, which still proves who is safe. [wiki] (snippet) — [Among Us Wiki: Visual tasks](https://among-us.fandom.com/wiki/Visual_tasks). Players have started Steam threads asking to "Remove medbay scan." [forum] — [Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/945360/discussions/0/3005551013340087014/)
- **Mind Scanners (The Outer Zone / Brave At Night, 2021):** you diagnose citizens with tool minigames and a Rorschach-style inkblot, picking one of three statements. It uses a retro-futuristic setting to keep away from real diagnoses and focuses "on the morality of diagnosis and how this power can be abused." [critic] (snippet) — [RPGFan review](https://www.rpgfan.com/review/mind-scanners/), [Slant review](https://www.slantmagazine.com/games/mind-scanners-review/) (not fetched individually)
- **I'm Not a Robot (Neal Agarwal, neal.fun, Sept 2025):** 48 CAPTCHA-parody levels, from level 1 "check a box" to Where's Waldo, drawing a 94%-accurate circle and day-trading. Played by more than 2.5 million people; Agarwal estimates "less than 1% have completed it." [critic] — [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91417204/im-not-a-robot). Level 48 is a video from CAPTCHA inventor Luis von Ahn congratulating you, after which you can download a PDF "certificate of humanity." [guide] (snippet) — [Dot Esports walkthrough](https://dotesports.com/word-games/news/im-not-a-robot-walkthrough-how-to-beat-all-levels), [Destructoid walkthrough](https://www.destructoid.com/how-to-beat-im-not-a-robot-complete-walkthrough-and-all-answers/)

### Inferences
- Fairness holds up best when the verdict comes from something the player can re-read: a needle, a printout, a phone answer, a hologram. It holds up worst when the tell is a body sign that innocents can also have and the game won't show which is which. For PoH, Rule 0 evidence should be one of: a video frame, the photo-vs-face comparison, the signature record, the vouch record, the duplicate-face registry hit, or the blink check. It should never be a "looks like a raccoon" judgement.
- No, I'm Not a Human's "humans may exhibit some of the traits" is the same look-alike idea as PoH's Dave. The difference: in No, I'm Not a Human the innocent explanation is narrative and unverifiable. In PoH it must be verifiable too. Dave's costume should come with evidence that clears him, e.g. the video frame shows the costume head off, or a human face matches the photo. The player then sees why the absurd-looking applicant passes.
- Blade Runner and No, I'm Not a Human both randomize who is non-human, so looks and names can't be memorized. PoH's "same seed, same week" is compatible with that, as long as validity is generated independently of costume or portrait (the "~half of absurd-looking applicants are valid" ratio).
- Among Us shows that a single decisive on-screen proof works so well that players in a deduction game ask to remove it. In an inspection game, that's exactly the property Rule 0 should have for Gary and the AI agent: once you look at the right evidence, there's no argument.
- I'm Not a Robot is the closest comedic precedent for "proving humanity." It starts with the trivial explicit version (check the box), escalates into absurd tests, and ends with a mock-official certificate of humanity. That's a ready-made shape for a PoH Rule 0 onboarding beat and a Humanity Day payoff.

### Gaps
- Could not find sources on "Do Not Feed the Monkeys", "Not Tonight" or "Contraband Police" relating to human-or-not checks. None of them has a human/non-human mechanic as far as the searches showed; "Not Tonight" is an ID-check bouncer game, but I found no design source to cite.
- No primary developer interview found for No, I'm Not a Human or Quarantine Zone explaining how the tells were designed. The "deliberate… fear" line is Wikipedia's wording.
- Critic reception of I'm Not a Robot's difficulty ("loses its charm when it becomes hard") appeared only in a search summary with an unclear source, so I left it out.

## 4. Introducing an "always implicitly true" rule, and using look-alikes so appearance never decides

### Takeaway
The pattern in the sources is to make the base rule the first, simplest explicit rule (Papers, Please Day 1: "Arstotzkans only"; I'm Not a Robot level 1: "check the box"). Later tells are revealed one per day through an in-world channel (No, I'm Not a Human's TV broadcast; Papers, Please's bulletin, never on a busy story morning). Look-alikes work only when a checkable document or instrument clears the innocent. In Papers, Please an appearance/sex mismatch only unlocks the scanner, and the scan decides.

### Cited Findings
- Papers, Please starts with one minimal rule on Day 1 (only Arstotzkan citizens) and grows from there. [wiki] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- Pope avoided introducing a bulletin rule on a day with a morning conversation, to protect player focus. [primary] — [Game Developer, 3 Sep 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)
- Pope planned early bulletins to explain systems ("I'll try to explain this quota system in an early bulletin"), and accepted that players "will probably miss important bulletin info no matter what," so the warning report must name the mistake. [primary] — [TIGSource Feb 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/)
- No, I'm Not a Human reveals one new identifying trait per day by TV broadcast, and you "can't test for certain things until your character learns about them." [wiki] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No,_I%27m_not_a_Human); [guide] — [TheGamer, 2026](https://www.thegamer.com/no-im-not-a-human-guests-visitor-human-symptoms-signs/)
- I'm Not a Robot's level 1 is the literal, trivial form of the implicit premise ("check a box to prove you're not a robot"). Later levels escalate. [critic] — [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91417204/im-not-a-robot)
- Papers, Please look-alike handling: "The scanner is also used to verify the sex of the entrant if their stated sex does not seem to match their appearance." Search only appears after the discrepancy is highlighted. [wiki] (snippet) — [Papers Please Wiki: Search](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Search)
- TNMN's resident folders are "always correct" and anchor the look-alike checks. Some real residents have "convincing excuses" for looking different. [guide] — [TheGamer, 2024](https://www.thegamer.com/thats-not-my-neighbor-how-to-play/)
- No, I'm Not a Human's innocent look-alikes (Grave Digger's dirty nails, red eyes from crying, the injured firefighter) are resolved by story context, not a document. That goes with forum reports of signs meaning "visitor to a specific character but human to another." [guide] — [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/no-im-not-a-human-guests-visitor-human-symptoms-signs/); [forum] — [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3180070/discussions/0/597412189643635876/)
- Blade Runner randomizes which characters are replicants, so casting and behaviour can't be trusted as a tell. [wiki] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner_(1997_video_game))

### Inferences
- For PoH, "Rule 0: the applicant must be human" works best printed at the top of the rulebook from Day 1, as a preamble that was "always true." The first case should be blatant and fully evidenced (e.g. Gary on Day 1, whose phrase video shows three heads, or three voices in the subtitle track). That mirrors Papers, Please's Day 1 simplicity and I'm Not a Robot's checkbox.
- Pope's pacing rule suggests Rule 0 shouldn't debut on the same day as a big story beat or a harder daily rule. If Day 1 already introduces "phrase in video," the Rule 0 debut should be the easiest possible read of that same video.
- Look-alikes need a clearing clue as visible as a guilty one. Dave the raccoon-costume human should have on-screen evidence that makes him valid (human face in the video frame or photo match, a normal blink). A player who challenges him then gets told why that was wrong, with the evidence named. This keeps "about half of absurd-looking applicants are valid" learnable instead of a coin flip.
- An AI agent applicant should fail Rule 0 through the same evidence channels (e.g. the video frame, the living/blink check, the duplicate face). Its remarks, name or window look shouldn't give it away, which keeps to the invariant.

### Gaps
- I found no source describing a game that retrofits an "always implicitly true" rule mid-game. The examples are all rules present from the start or revealed daily.
- No designer quote found on how many look-alike innocents to include, or on the ratio of suspicious-looking valid entrants, in any of these games.

## 5. Comedy design: keeping an always-catchable gag character funny

### Takeaway
Jorji stays the favourite in Papers, Please because each visit is a new attempt shaped by that day's rules: no passport, then an invented country, then a real passport with the wrong permit, then a ticket from a rule that has expired. He's charming and cheerful about losing, and his arc pays off (correct papers on Day 11, later help to the player). Comedy craft says the same: vary each repeat, escalate, and subvert on the third beat. Beyond that the repetition goes stale.

### Inferences
- Jorji is funny even though you always catch him because the question each visit is "what did he try this time, and which rule does it hit?", not "will he fool me?" Gary should work the same way. Each day's disguise answers yesterday's rule (fixing the clue that caught him) and walks into today's. The Rule 0 evidence shows up through the newest rule's check, so it's a new reveal each day and not the same "he's raccoons" beat.
- The Vincent Adultman joke is that everyone except one character is oblivious. In PoH the player always sees through Gary, so the obliviousness should come from the institution: a clerk remark in the rulebook, a form that addresses him as "Mr. Gary" with a straight face, or a Gazette item. The player's evidence should never be what's in doubt. This fits the deadpan tone and the rule that no jokes sit in logic files, since all of it can live in `src/content/`.
- Rule-of-three pacing suggests varying Gary's approach every day and saving a structural surprise for the last day (e.g. papers that are perfect by every other rule, so Rule 0 alone catches him). This replaces Jorji's "finally legitimate" payoff, which Rule 0 rules out.
- Jorji's charm (admits he's wrong, praises the inspector for catching him) is a tone model for Gary's rejection lines. Being a good sport about losing keeps an always-rejected character likeable rather than pitiable.
- Since about half of absurd-looking applicants are valid (Dave), Gary must be caught by evidence and not by costume. Otherwise Dave teaches the player that raccoons are fine and Gary becomes a trap. Pairing Dave and Gary on the same day with the evidence visibly differing would be a clean teaching beat.
