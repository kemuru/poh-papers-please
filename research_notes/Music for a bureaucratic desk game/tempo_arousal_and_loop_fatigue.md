# Music tempo, arousal and task performance; listener fatigue in looping game music

Context for the writer: the target is a browser desk game (read documents, stamp them, 6-minute shift clock, 5 to 60 minutes per session). Current music is Eno-style ambient (slow bells and choir, no beat, notes every few seconds) and players call it sleepy. An earlier 8-bar march loop grated within minutes: its melody came back every 27 s, it resolved at the loop point, and it had a click on every beat.

## Q1. What does research say about background music's tempo, mode, loudness, complexity and lyrics on arousal, alertness and performance in focused, repetitive, reading-heavy tasks? Does very slow ambient music lower arousal?

### Takeaway
The best-supported model is "arousal-mood plus capacity". Tempo mainly drives arousal and mode mainly drives mood, and both can help performance. At the same time, music uses attention, and the cost grows with loudness, event density, complexity and above all intelligible lyrics. For reading, instrumental music at low to moderate level costs almost nothing. The only condition that clearly hurt reading comprehension was fast AND loud. Slow tempo (60 BPM) and pauses measurably lower arousal. In monotonous vigilance tasks people sit on the low side of the arousal curve, and music that raises arousal cuts mind-wandering. So the players' "sleepy" complaint is what the literature predicts.

### Cited Findings

**Arousal-mood hypothesis (the core studies)**
- Thompson, Schellenberg & Husain (2001), *Psychological Science* 12:248–251. Participants did a spatial task after hearing a Mozart sonata (pleasant, energetic), an Albinoni adagio (slow, sad), or silence. Performance beat silence only after the Mozart. The two pieces produced different enjoyment, arousal and mood, and the authors conclude the "Mozart effect" is an artifact of arousal and mood. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11437309/); [SAGE DOI](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9280.00345)
- Husain, Thompson & Schellenberg (2002), *Music Perception* 20(2):151–171. The same Mozart sonata (K. 448, played at about 110 BPM) was re-rendered via MIDI at 165 BPM (fast) and 60 BPM (slow), in major and minor. Listeners heard at least 10 minutes at about 60–70 dB. — [author PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/Husain.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1525/mp.2002.20.2.151)
  - Tempo had a large effect on arousal, F(1,32) = 91.82, p < .001. Arousal *increased* after the fast versions and *decreased* (negative difference scores) after the slow ones. Mode did not affect arousal. — [Husain PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/Husain.pdf)
  - Mode affected mood (major better), F(1,32) = 17.16, p < .001. Tempo did not affect mood. Spatial scores were best after fast-major music. The music manipulations explained about 12% of extra variance in spatial scores. The POMS "Fatigue-Inertia" subscale did not vary with tempo or mode. — [Husain PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/Husain.pdf)
- Bernardi, Porta & Sleight (2006), *Heart* 92:445–452, n = 24 (12 musicians, 12 controls). Faster tempi and simpler rhythms raised ventilation, blood pressure and heart rate over baseline, with no habituation. A 2-minute **pause (silence) lowered heart rate, blood pressure and minute ventilation "even below baseline"**. Conclusion: "Music induces an arousal effect, predominantly related to the tempo. Slow or meditative music can induce a relaxing effect; relaxation is particularly evident during a pause." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16199412/)

**Tempo × loudness on reading comprehension**
- Thompson, Schellenberg & Letnic (2012), *Psychology of Music* 40(6). Mozart piano (Sonata for Two Pianos in D) in four conditions: slow/soft (110 BPM, 60 dB), slow/loud (110 BPM, 72.4 dB), fast/soft (150 BPM, 60 dB), fast/loud (150 BPM, 72.4 dB). A pilot showed that the 110→150 BPM change is psychologically equal to 60→72.4 dB. n = 25, with 4 minutes of reading and six questions per condition. — [author PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf); [DOI](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735611400173)
  - Mean correct answers out of 6: silence baseline 2.77 (SD 0.85), slow/soft 2.38, slow/loud 2.43, **fast/soft 2.96 (best, slightly above silence)**, **fast/loud 1.95 (the only condition significantly below baseline**, t(39) = 2.37, p < .05). Tempo × intensity interaction F(1,23) = 8.14, p < .01. Loudness had no effect at slow tempo. — [author PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)
  - Authors' mechanism: fast music has "a greater number of auditory events per unit time that are difficult to ignore because of greater intensity". "Slow-tempo music may allow for continuous and spontaneous recovery from acoustic interference." They also suggest the costs of music for reading "may be offset by the benefits of music listening for mood and arousal". — [author PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)

**Meta-analyses: reading and lyrics**
- Kämpfe, Sedlmeier & Renkewitz (2011), *Psychology of Music*. The global effect of background music is null, "most probably due to averaging out specific effects". Against no music, background music disturbs reading, has small detrimental effects on memory, and improves emotional reactions and sports performance. — [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ944201); [DOI](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0305735610376261)
- Kämpfe's reading-only estimate was r = −0.11 (d = −0.22), from only 8 studies (as reported by Vasilev et al.). — [Vasilev et al. 2018 PDF](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/29995/9/1745691617747398.pdf)
- Vasilev, Kirkby & Angele (2018), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 13(5):567–597, a Bayesian meta-analysis of 65 studies. Reading comprehension overall g = −0.21. Speech g = −0.26, noise g = −0.17, music g = −0.19 ("fairly small"). Effects on reading speed and proofreading were very small, and their credible intervals included 0. — [PDF](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/29995/9/1745691617747398.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691617747398)
  - **Lyrical music was more distracting than non-lyrical music** (95% probability, difference g = −0.19). It was as distracting as intelligible speech (only 54% probability of any difference). For **non-lyrical music the effect estimate was "very close to 0"**, no different from plain acoustic noise. — [PDF](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/29995/9/1745691617747398.pdf)

**Complexity, volume and the performer**
- Gonzalez & Aiello (2019), *J. Exp. Psych.: Applied* 25(3):431–444. The same instrumental piece was used, with complexity set by the number of instrumental layers and volume at soft 50–56 dB or loud 62–78 dB. Music generally *impaired* a complex word-pair task. On a simple vigilance-like task ("finding As"), **complex music gave the highest scores** (M = 97.52 vs simple music 91.31 vs silence 91.00; F(1,129) = 5.25, p = .02, ηp² = .04). Loud music trended the same way (96.90 vs soft 91.93, p = .08). Boredom proneness (preference for external stimulation) moderated both effects. — [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/2019-gonzalez.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000202)
- Personality: introverts are hurt more than extraverts by background music. With pop music, Furnham & Bradley (1997) found introverts did worse on delayed recall and reading comprehension. Furnham & Strbac (2002) found introverts did worse at reading comprehension with music or noise but not in silence. In Furnham & Allass (1999), introverts declined steadily from silence to simple to complex music, while extraverts did best with complex music. Replications are inconsistent (Furnham et al. 1999 and Avila et al. did not support the theory), and the EEG-arousal basis of Eysenck's account is disputed. — [Küssner 2017 mini-review, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01991); [Furnham & Bradley 1997](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/(SICI)1099-0720(199710)11:5%3C445::AID-ACP472%3E3.0.CO;2-R)

**Vigilance and sustained attention**
- Kiss & Linnell (online 2020, print 2021), *Psychological Research* ("The effect of preferred background music on task-focus in sustained attention"). n = 40, a Psychomotor Vigilance Task in 5 blocks of about 6 minutes each, with self-selected music averaging **112.6 BPM (SD 32.4)**. Music raised task-focus (0.62 vs 0.54) and cut mind-wandering (0.18 vs 0.27) but did not change overall RT. Task-focus fell over blocks with or without music. The authors place a low-demand task on the hypo-aroused side of the inverted-U, where music's stimulation helps. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8357712/)
- A 2024 *Scientific Reports* study ran two online experiments (n = 106 and n = 77) of a PVT with self-chosen playlists, averaging **116.5 BPM (SD 14.1) and 117.6 BPM (SD 28.9)**. Music gave faster RTs (about 8 ms; b = 7.785, p = .047 and b = 8.100, p = .030), less mind-wandering and more task-focus (all p ≤ .005). It also *increased* low-arousal "external distraction" states. Mood and arousal both went up and statistically mediated the effects (serial mood→arousal mediation on RT of about −8 to −10 ms). The authors cite the inverted-U: "only an intermediate arousal level is linked to maximum performance." — [Scientific Reports / PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11045806/)

**Is slow ambient music sedating?**
- Brian Eno's own liner notes for *Ambient 1: Music for Airports* say ambient music "is intended to induce calm and a space to think" and must be "as ignorable as it is interesting". He wanted "a silence at least twice as long as the sound", built from tape loops of different lengths phasing against each other. — [Wikipedia summary of liner notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_1:_Music_for_Airports)
- Scarratt et al. (2023), *PLOS ONE*, analysed 225,626 tracks from Spotify sleep playlists. Against general music, sleep music is much softer (Cohen's d = −1.25 loudness), lower-energy (d = −1.46), more acoustic (d = +1.20), more instrumental (d = +1.10), lower-valence (d = −0.93) and slower (d = −0.47). The largest cluster (117,237 tracks) was "ambient" (meditation/drone). — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0278813)
- A companion paper by the same group is titled "Music that is used while studying and music that is used for sleep share similar musical features, genres and subgroups". — [Scientific Reports 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-31692-8)

### Inferences
- The Eno-style score (no beat, one event every few seconds, long decays, lots of silence) sits in the audio-feature space of sleep playlists (Scarratt) and in the "slow tempo lowers arousal" condition (Husain 60 BPM; Bernardi pauses). The genre is designed to "induce calm" (Eno). A repetitive stamping task is exactly the low-arousal, mind-wandering-prone situation where the vigilance studies found arousal-raising music helpful. The sleepiness complaint therefore has a clear mechanism. (Inference.)
- A reasonable target is a perceptible pulse in the **~100–120 BPM** zone, which is the tempo people choose for their own focus music in the vigilance studies. Keep **note density and loudness low**. The one clearly harmful condition was fast plus loud (Thompson 2012), and fast plus soft was numerically the best. (Inference from the studies above. The exact BPM window is not tested directly.)
- Because reading is the core verb, **avoid intelligible vocals and lyrics**. A wordless "ooh/aah" choir is probably fine, but syllabic or text-like vocals are not (Vasilev). (Inference. The meta-analysis did not separate wordless vocals.)
- Major or bright modal harmony supports mood without changing arousal (Husain). A deadpan satirical tone can still sit in major/mixolydian with a dry timbre. (Inference.)
- Individual differences matter a lot (introverts, boredom proneness, liking). This supports a music volume slider and an "off" option. It also supports a "focus" variant with fewer layers. (Inference.)

### Gaps
- No study found that tests "Eno-style ambient" or beatless drone specifically against a light-pulse score on a reading task. The sedation argument is indirect (tempo→arousal, pause→relaxation, sleep-playlist features).
- Kämpfe (2011) sub-effect sizes (memory, emotion, sports) were not retrieved in numeric form. The full paper is paywalled.
- Almost all tempo studies use classical piano or pop. None use sparse synthetic game textures, and most play 4–10 minute excerpts, not 60-minute sessions.
- A 2024 systematic review on music and auditory stimulation, autonomic arousal and attention ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167876024000321)) returned 403 and was not read.

## Q2. What does research say about music in games and player performance/experience, including tempo and time pressure (racing, puzzle, Tetris, timers)?

### Takeaway
Faster or more arousing music makes players act faster and less carefully. It raises speed and errors in driving games and stress hormones in action games. In Tetris, tempo effects on overall score are weak or null, but accelerating music synced to game speed may hurt novices. Liking the music predicts better performance and experience. For a clerk game where accuracy matters, the evidence argues against big tempo ramps as the main escalation tool.

### Cited Findings
- Brodsky (2001), *Transportation Research Part F*. In a driving simulator, as background-music tempo rose, simulated speed and perceived speed rose. Virtual red-light violations, lane crossings and collisions were most frequent with fast music. The proposed mechanism is attentional competition from "the greater number of temporal events which must be processed". — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1369847801000250); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-effects-of-music-tempo-on-simulated-driving-and-Brodsky/65acac8e4d105da4f18417b2c58a3ae3855db755)
- Cassidy & MacDonald (2010), *Scandinavian J. Psychology* 51:455–464. n = 70, a driving game in 7 conditions including experimenter-chosen high- or low-arousal music at 70 or 130 BPM. "Increasing the tempo of experimenter-selected music resulted in faster performance and increased inaccuracy for high-arousal music." Experimenter-selected music produced the poorest performance and experience. Self-selected music helped accuracy and experience but led to overestimating elapsed time. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9450.2010.00830.x)
- Hébert et al. (2005), *Life Sciences* 76:2371–2380. Players of a shooting game for 10 minutes with the built-in (pop/techno) music had significantly higher salivary cortisol 15 minutes after play than players in silence. The authors take this as evidence that "the auditory input contributes significantly to the stress response". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lfs.2004.11.011); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15748630/)
- Hufschmitt, Cardon & Jacopin (IEEE CoG 2020) built "Sonotris", Tetris with piano/celesta music that accelerates with game speed up to 12× the initial tempo (the tempo halves while the subdivision doubles, so the perceived acceleration feels continuous). n = 41. Across the 5 tempo presets there was **no significant difference in final level (p = .809)**, which "confirms Lawrence's results". Players who liked the music reached higher levels (p = .021). Only marginal differences appeared for "neutral" music-raters (52% of players), who did worse when tempo rose each line and pre-empted the next speed-up (p = .053 / .068). — [CoG paper 217](https://ieee-cog.org/2020/papers/paper_217.pdf); [CoG paper 224](https://ieee-cog.org/2020/papers/paper_224.pdf)
  - The same group's abstracts claim that "a synchronous and steadily increasing tempo makes the game difficult for novice players". They say "a synchronous and increasing tempo can increase player stress and degrade performance, while tempo that is out of sync with game actions or slowed down regularly can defuse stress", and that tempo effects occur without players noticing (44 players and 230 games, then 19 players and 50 games). — [ACM IUI 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3397481.3450684); [ACM CHI PLAY 2020 EA](https://doi.org/10.1145/3383668.3419934)
- Wharton & Collins (2011), *Game Studies* 11(2), a pilot with n = 15 over about 45 minutes of *Fallout 3*. Fast-tempo music made players "more aggressive" and "less cautious". Slow music made them "less active", as if "the game were in slow motion". Players could not reliably pick their own songs to raise immersion or enjoyment. The paper also notes that "the repetitive nature of game music meant that many players would turn the sound off altogether." — [Game Studies](https://gamestudies.org/1102/articles/wharton_collins)
- Mario's timer escalation: *Super Mario Bros.*'s overworld theme runs at about 100 BPM. "When the player has less than 100 units of time left to complete the stage, the music's tempo accelerates." — [Wikipedia: Super Mario Bros. theme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Bros._theme). The background music pauses for a short "Hurry Up" fanfare and then resumes sped up. The motif recurs in later Mario games and on Mario Kart's final lap. — [Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Hurry_Up) (search-result summary; the page itself is a disambiguation stub)

### Inferences
- In a stamp-accuracy game, a strong tempo ramp near the deadline trades accuracy for speed (Brodsky; Cassidy & MacDonald), which may be the intended feeling. The evidence on stress (Hébert; Hufschmitt) suggests escalating with **density and layers rather than large tempo jumps**. If tempo rises, keep the change modest, and consider not locking it to the shift clock, since "desynchronised" tempo defused stress in Sonotris. (Inference. The Sonotris evidence is weak.)
- The Mario pattern (a short one-shot warning, then a faster version) is a proven, readable signal. It works best as a brief discrete cue at a threshold, such as 60 s left, rather than continuous acceleration. (Inference.)
- Liking predicts performance (Sonotris; Cassidy & MacDonald), so music that players don't mind hearing matters more than tuning the exact BPM. (Inference.)

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on music in document-reading or clerical *games* specifically.
- Lawrence's (2012) master's thesis on tempo and game performance was not read directly; its null result is known only through Hufschmitt et al.
- The Hébert game title and exact cortisol values were not retrieved (abstract only).
- The Sonotris effects are marginal (p ≈ .05–.07) with small samples, so treat them as suggestive.

## Q3. What does game-audio practice and scholarship say about listener fatigue and repetition in looping music: loop lengths, time to fatigue, loop-point resolution, strong melodies, rests and silence, dynamic range?

### Takeaway
Practitioners agree that fatigue comes from recognisable landmarks: a memorable melody and a strong cadence at the loop seam. Short loops heard many times make it worse. Remedies are long material (Phillips: at least about 5 minutes before literal repetition), seams that don't resolve, low-salience textures, melody-free passages, and planned silence (Minecraft: 10–20 minutes between tracks; Halo-style fade-outs). Hard data on how many minutes or loops before fatigue sets in is essentially absent. Most of the numbers are practitioner opinion.

### Cited Findings
- Winifred Phillips (*A Composer's Guide to Game Music*, MIT Press 2014) discusses "several ways of composing music that can loop repeatedly without fatiguing listeners — slow textures, perpetual development, and variations." — [MAKE review](https://www.makemag.com/double-review-composers-guide-to-game-music-and-sound-play/)
- Phillips (as cited by Cook) says musical variety helps avoid "repetition fatigue" (pp. 66–67). She recommends an "answer/question" structure, "in which the second half of a phrase or section is used as an introduction and the first half as the conclusion leading naturally back to the beginning" (pp. 168 ff.). In other words, the loop seam should not sit at a cadence. — [Cook, *Music Theory Online* 29.3 (2023)](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.23.29.3/mto.23.29.3.cook.html)
- Phillips, as quoted secondhand: "one way to avoid ear fatigue is for a single piece of music to be hitting at least 5 minutes in length before looping around again." — [Audient tutorial](https://audient.com/tutorial/composing-music-video-games/) (secondary source; the page number was not verified)
- Karen Collins (2007, as cited by Cook) lists 8-bit loop-disguise strategies. They include an introductory section outside the repeating macroloop, "looping just the last section of a theme, for example an ABCD form in which just the D section repeats", and repeating a loop at a different pitch level with changed rhythm. — [Cook, MTO](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.23.29.3/mto.23.29.3.cook.html)
- Hunt (2020) analysed 21,391 game-music pieces across consoles. On average **62.79% (SD 3.49) of musical clips are repeats of earlier clips**, and this held roughly constant across hardware generations. Song *length* grew with storage. — [Hunt, CSMC/MuMe 2020](https://boblsturm.github.io/aimusic2020/papers/CSMC__MuMe_2020_paper_10.pdf)
- Marcelo Martins (a Game Developer blog, opinion, no data) offers a fatigue scale: 1 play ideal, 2 tolerable, 3–5 "increasingly problematic", 5+ "severe listener repulsion". He argues music length should match or exceed typical gameplay time on that screen. He also says melodies are the most memory-intensive element. Techniques he names: reorder sections, alternate versions with and without the main melody (*Zelda: Skyward Sword*), **fade to silence after about 3 repetitions (Halo)**, random playlists without back-to-back repeats, and 30-second ambient "expandable endings". — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/rethinking-the-audio-loop-in-games)
- Minecraft's in-game music "randomly plays 10 to 20 minutes after currently playing tracks are finished". Track lengths run from 1:05 to 15:03, and Java Edition has a "Music Frequency" setting. — [Minecraft Wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Music). C418 described wanting the music to feel "as if it were already there" and was interested in the relation between silence and sound (unsourced interviews). — [Codelearn](https://codelearn.com/blog/music-in-minecraft-between-silence-and-the-unexpected/)
- *Zelda: Breath of the Wild* has little or no continuous overworld music, with sparse piano phrases meant to accentuate ambient sound. Sound director Hajime Wakai explained the logic of having no music in the open world. — [GoNintendo](https://gonintendo.com/stories/325951-zelda-breath-of-the-wild-sound-designer-on-finding-the-right-mus); [Nintendo Everything](https://nintendoeverything.com/breath-of-the-wild-composers-on-changing-up-zeldas-music-formula-and-more/) (search-snippet level)
- Sound designer Bjørn Jacobsen on repetition: "If a step stands out or is in any way distinguishable from the others, then the player … will automatically start to take note of this pattern." Layered randomisation turns 5 variations per layer into 125 (or 625) combinations, but "the more variations we added, the more sensitive the system became to odd samples." In a small demo, players who heard out-of-key sound effects quit a minute or two earlier (anecdotal, n ≈ 19). — [A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/game-audio-immersion/)
- Players historically turned game music off because of repetition, and games now let music be muted separately. — [Wharton & Collins 2011](https://gamestudies.org/1102/articles/wharton_collins)
- Physiology of rests: silence between music segments lowers heart rate, blood pressure and ventilation below baseline. — [Bernardi et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600)

### Inferences
- The failed march fits every red flag. At 27 s per loop there are about 13 exact repeats per 6-minute shift, far past the "3–5" threshold. It had a memorable melody on every pass, a cadence at the seam (a landmark), and a click on every beat, which raised event density and salience, the factor Thompson 2012 and Brodsky blame for distraction. (Inference.)
- For 5–60 minute sessions in 6-minute shifts, aim for **no literal repeat of any melodic phrase within a shift**, or at most once. Treat the melody as a rare event: somewhere between once every 1–2 minutes and once per shift. Between melodic statements, play texture/pulse layers. (Inference from Phillips' 5-minute guidance, Martins' repetition scale and melody-muting practice.)
- Silence helps against fatigue, but Bernardi shows silence also relaxes. In a time-pressured shift, "rests" should usually be *drops to a light pulse or bed layer* rather than full silence. Save true silence for between shifts, the pay screen or night screens. Minecraft-length gaps (10–20 min) exceed a whole shift and would read as "no music". (Inference.)

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that measures *when* (minutes or loop count) players start to find a game loop fatiguing. A widely repeated "90–120 seconds before the ear notices a loop" figure traces only to an AI-tool blog ([sorceress.games](https://sorceress.games/blog/layer-how-to-make-good-video-game-music-ai-loops-2026)), so treat it as unreliable.
- Phillips' book could not be accessed directly (1library returned 403). Her quotes here come through reviews, Cook (MTO) and Audient.
- No sources found from "Designing Music NOW" (Guy Whitmore) or from GDC talks specific to loop fatigue in this pass.
- Nothing quantitative found on dynamic range (LUFS or crest-factor targets) for background game music against fatigue.
- The music-psychology literature on repetition and liking (mere exposure, inverted-U liking curves) was not searched.

## Q4. Which techniques avoid repetition and tie music to play: generative/procedural systems, vertical layering, horizontal resequencing, stingers, timer tension layers, phrase-level variation, harmonic rhythm?

### Takeaway
Three families are mature and cheap enough for a browser game. **Vertical layering** adds or removes synced stems with intensity. **Horizontal resequencing** randomly orders short segments with beat or bar-aligned transitions. **Rule-based generation** is the Mini Metro / Spore / No Man's Sky approach: authored note pools and rhythms steered by game data, with player actions quantised into the music. Eno's incommensurate loop lengths are a very cheap generative trick.

### Cited Findings
- **Vertical layering (Phillips, GDC 2021).** "Multiple independent audio files simultaneously … stacks these layers on top of one another in perfect synchronization". The LittleBigPlanet franchise uses a "six-layered system". In *Spyder*, "sometimes gamers only hear the rhythm elements … sometimes only the synths … sometimes the whole mix." Vertical layering "can instill a sense of progression and variety without needing to fragment the composition." — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/pure-vertical-layering-for-game-music-composers-from-spyder-to-sackboy-gdc-2021-)
- **Horizontal resequencing (Phillips, GDC 2021).** Segments carry markers "positioned on each individual beat, at the beginning of each measure, or at designated points" for switching. In *Spyder*, "every level of the game had over thirty music segments" and "the order of those segments is randomized so that players can't anticipate what they'll hear", which "can keep game music from coming across as repetitive." — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/horizontal-resequencing-and-dynamic-transitions-for-game-music-composers-from-spyder-to-sackboy-gdc-2021-)
- **Gameplay-generated music (Mini Metro, Disasterpeace / Rich Vreeland).** Nearly all audio is "samples that are triggered in real-time as a result of player actions". Each metro line is a pulse sequence (one pulse per station). Changing a line "replaces the oldest note in the harmonic structure with the next one in line" and moves the rhythm to the next in a list. Passenger events are fanned out as arpeggios, with different passenger shapes staggered on the hour, half hour and quarter hours. Sample envelopes get longer as the game clock slows. The engine updates on musical subdivisions such as quintuplets or 16ths. Hard caps (7 lines, 4 trains per line) keep density bounded. — [Designing Sound interview](https://designingsound.org/2016/02/18/the-programmed-music-of-mini-metro-interview-with-rich-vreeland-disasterpeace/). The system combines data sonification with serialism and avoids looping music tracks entirely. — [Disasterpeace talk page](https://disasterpeace.com/blog/mini-metro.serialism-talk.html)
- **Spore (Brian Eno with Kent Jolly and Aaron McLeran).** Prototyped in Max/MSP, then built in Pure Data from many small samples, and generated in real time. — [GDC Vault: Procedural Music in SPORE](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/323/Procedural-Music-in); [Wikipedia: Generative music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_music) (search-snippet level)
- **No Man's Sky (Paul Weir's "Pulse").** A generative system that recombines a large library of 65daysofstatic stems and fragments in real time. The band's album acts as a "music bible". — [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/gaming/no-mans-sky-music/); [Audiokinetic Q&A](https://www.audiokinetic.com/en/blog/behind-the-sound-of-no-mans-sky-a-qa-with-paul-weir-on-procedural-audio/) (search-snippet level; the full pages did not load)
- **Incommensurate loops (Eno).** *Music for Airports* runs tape loops of different lengths that "configure in the way they chose to configure". Eno wanted "complicated rather than simple relationships" and "a silence at least twice as long as the sound." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_1:_Music_for_Airports)
- **Syncing actions to the beat (Kondo).** In the Super Mario Bros. theme, "the character's movements and players' button presses sync with the beat". Kondo rejected drafts that did not "accentuate the action" or "harmonize with the sound effects well enough." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Bros._theme)
- **Timer tension (Mario).** A one-shot fanfare at the threshold, then faster background music. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Bros._theme); [Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Hurry_Up)
- **Phrase-level disguise.** Answer/question seams (Phillips), transposed repeats and loop-only-the-last-section (Collins). — [Cook, MTO](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.23.29.3/mto.23.29.3.cook.html). Other practitioner devices are instrumentation changes on each pass, added countermelodies, and sequencing a phrase at new pitch levels instead of repeating it. — [Intermittent Mechanism blog](https://intermittentmechanism.blog/2023/04/12/loops-in-video-game-music/) (unsourced opinion)

### Inferences
- A browser-friendly design that fits these sources (inference):
  - **Bed layer, always on during the shift.** A soft pulse at ~104–116 BPM (for example muted plucks or a typewriter-ish tick at low level on off-beats, *not* a click on every beat), with slow harmonic rhythm (one chord per 2–4 bars).
  - **Texture layers.** Two to four stems that fade in and out every 16–32 bars at random, or with applicant load. Use Eno-style incommensurate cycle lengths, for example 7, 9 and 11 bars, so the combined pattern takes hundreds of bars to recur. (7×9×11 = 693 bars ≈ 25 min at 110 BPM in 4/4.)
  - **Melody as an event.** Draw 4–8 short motif fragments (2–4 bars) from a pool, with transposition or rhythm variation, and place them sparsely. Never end a fragment on the tonic at a seam; end on the 2nd, 5th or a suspended chord (answer/question).
  - **Stingers on player actions.** Stamp sounds pitched to the current chord and quantised to the next 8th or 16th, in the Mini Metro and Kondo manner. APPROVED and DENIED can use different chord tones. This gives immediate feedback and turns the player's work into the music.
  - **Seeded RNG for the variation.** This keeps "same seed, same week" true for music too, if desired.

### Gaps
- No primary technical sources were retrieved for No Man's Sky Pulse rules or Spore's Pure Data patches, so their parameters are unknown.
- No research found on the perceptual effect of harmonic-rhythm choices on fatigue. The "slow harmonic rhythm" advice above is inference.
- No Markov-chain or rule-based variation studies measuring player fatigue were found.

## Q5. What concrete parameters are recommended for "alert but not stressful" desk work: tempo, note density, melody frequency, phrase and silence lengths, and last-minute tension escalation?

### Takeaway
No source gives a validated parameter set for this use case. The ranges below are **inferences** assembled from the cited findings: moderate tempo (~100–120 BPM pulse), low loudness and density, no lyrics, major/bright harmony, and melody used sparingly and never looped verbatim within a shift. Rests should be "thinning" rather than true silence during the shift. The last minute escalates by adding a layer and a one-shot cue plus a small tempo lift, not by a big accelerando.

### Cited Findings (anchors for the numbers)
- 60 BPM lowered arousal and 165 BPM raised it. Tempo affected arousal, not mood. — [Husain et al. 2002](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/Husain.pdf)
- 150 BPM at 60 dB was the best reading condition, and 150 BPM at 72.4 dB was the only harmful one. 110→150 BPM is perceptually equal to +12.4 dB. — [Thompson et al. 2012](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)
- People's self-chosen focus music averaged about 113–118 BPM, and it reduced mind-wandering in vigilance tasks. — [Kiss & Linnell 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8357712/); [Sci Rep 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11045806/)
- Soft (50–56 dB) versus loud (62–78 dB) volume mattered less than layering complexity on a simple task, and music hurt complex tasks. — [Gonzalez & Aiello 2019](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/2019-gonzalez.pdf)
- Non-lyrical music has about zero effect on reading, while lyrical music ≈ speech (g ≈ −0.19 worse). — [Vasilev et al. 2018](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/29995/9/1745691617747398.pdf)
- Silence and pauses drive physiology below baseline, which is relaxing. — [Bernardi et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600)
- At least about 5 minutes before a literal loop (Phillips, secondhand). — [Audient](https://audient.com/tutorial/composing-music-video-games/). Fade out after about 3 repetitions (Halo, per Martins). — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/rethinking-the-audio-loop-in-games)
- Faster tempo means faster but less accurate play, with more stress. A steady synced accelerando may hurt novices, and desynced or periodically slowed tempo defuses stress. — [Cassidy & MacDonald 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9450.2010.00830.x); [Brodsky 2001](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1369847801000250); [Hufschmitt et al. 2020](https://ieee-cog.org/2020/papers/paper_217.pdf); [Hébert et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lfs.2004.11.011)
- Mario's approach: about 100 BPM base, then a fanfare and speed-up under 100 time units. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Bros._theme)

### Inferences (proposed parameters, all inference)
- **Tempo.** A felt pulse of **100–120 BPM** (e.g. 108). Avoid under ~80 BPM without a pulse (sedating) and over ~140 BPM (speed and error risk).
- **Note density.** Bed layer at about 2–4 soft onsets per beat at most, and never a hard transient on every beat. Total audible onsets across layers should average roughly 2–6 per second. Leave space in the 1–4 kHz speech/consonant band for UI and voice blips. (No direct source for these numbers. They extrapolate the "events per unit time" mechanism in Thompson 2012 and Brodsky 2001.)
- **Loudness.** Music sits clearly under the stamp and UI sounds. If treated like Thompson's "soft" condition, keep it about 10–12 dB below the SFX peaks. Offer a slider and a separate music mute.
- **Mode and harmony.** Major, mixolydian or lydian colour for mood. Slow harmonic rhythm (a chord every 2–4 bars). Avoid perfect cadences at segment seams.
- **Melody frequency.** A motif fragment (2–4 bars ≈ 4.5–9 s at 108 BPM) about every 45–90 s, drawn from a pool of at least 6 variants. The same fragment should not repeat within one shift (6 min ≈ 160 bars). About 60–70% of the time should have no lead melody.
- **Phrase and segment lengths.** Segments of 8–16 bars (18–36 s), resequenced at random under a seed. Layer cycles of coprime bar lengths (7/9/11/13) so the full texture never audibly repeats within a session.
- **Rests.** In-shift rests of 4–8 bars (9–18 s) where the melody and upper layers drop out but the pulse bed stays. True silence only between shifts and on menus or pay screens. If sessions reach 30–60 min, consider a 20–40 s music-free stretch every ~10 min, or on alternate shifts, as a Halo/Minecraft-style reset.
- **Stingers.** A stamp chord-tone hit quantised to the next 16th (≤ ~140 ms latency at 108 BPM), from a pool of at least 3–5 variants per outcome.
- **Last minute of the shift clock.**
  - At T−60 s: a single short cue (1–2 bars) plus one added layer, such as a ticking hi-hat or pizzicato ostinato at 8ths.
  - A small tempo lift of about +5–10% (108→116 BPM), or none. Optionally stay slightly desynchronised from the clock countdown.
  - At T−15 s: more density (16ths in the tick layer) and a rising filter or pitch. Keep the loudness increase ≤ 3 dB.
  - At T−0: cut to a dry end stinger, followed by silence at the day's end.
  - Avoid a continuous accelerando locked to the clock (Sonotris) and big loud/fast jumps (Thompson; Brodsky).

### Gaps
- Every numeric parameter in this section is a design extrapolation. None has been validated with players on this game or any comparable reading-and-stamp game. Playtest A/B comparisons (for example 108 BPM pulse versus the current ambient score, measured by accuracy per shift, time per applicant and self-reported sleepiness) would be needed to confirm.
- No research found on the ideal ratio of silence to music for time-pressured tasks, or on dB-offset conventions between music and SFX in desk or puzzle games.
