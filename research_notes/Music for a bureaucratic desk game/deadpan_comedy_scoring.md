## 1. Alexandre Desplat (and Mark Mothersbaugh) for Wes Anderson: instruments, rhythmic devices, tempi, how deadpan and whimsy are made

### Takeaway
The Anderson sound is a small, dry, mostly treble chamber band: plucked strings (balalaika, banjo, mandolin, harpsichord, pizzicato), struck metal (cimbalom, glockenspiel, bells, celesta-like music-box colours), one instrument per part, very precise repeating rhythms, and a melody that stays audible and a little melancholy. It is deadpan because it is strict and "metronomically precise", and whimsical because the instrument colours are odd, folkloric or toy-like, not because it reacts to jokes.

### Cited Findings
**Grand Budapest Hotel (2014)**
- Instrument palette: "zithers, Hungarian cimbaloms, Gregorian chants, Alpen horns, whistlers, Russian balalaikas, organs, bells and even yodelers" — [Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2014/03/26/the-grand-budapest-hotel-alexandre-desplat/)
- The core is a 50-person group of French and Russian balalaika players, with members of the Osipov State Russian Folk Orchestra, plus cimbaloms, zithers, alphorns and sampled folk recordings — [Wikipedia: Grand Budapest Hotel (soundtrack)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grand_Budapest_Hotel_(soundtrack))
- Desplat: "Mitteleuropa...goes from Switzerland to Turkey... zither, balalaikas, percussion...Alpenhorns. When you start mixing them together it becomes kind of a strange, special, weird sound." — [Wikipedia: GBH soundtrack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grand_Budapest_Hotel_(soundtrack))
- The score is described as having "metronomically precise rhythms and almost child-like music box instrumental ideas" — [Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2014/03/26/the-grand-budapest-hotel-alexandre-desplat/); same phrase quoted in [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grand_Budapest_Hotel_(soundtrack))
- Main (Moustafa) theme: "an elegant dance for a cimbalom underpinned by humming balalaikas". Variants: church organ ("A Prayer for Madame D"); stand-up bass and brushed snares, jazzy ("The New Lobby Boy"); snares imitating a steam-train rhythm ("Night Train to Nebelsbad"); a male voice choir singing the nonsense syllables "rum-te-tum-te-tum"; glockenspiels and timpani hits — [Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2014/03/26/the-grand-budapest-hotel-alexandre-desplat/)
- "Mr. Moustafa": listed at 150 BPM (also 75 BPM as the half-time reading), key of C major — [Tunebat](https://tunebat.com/Info/Mr-Moustafa-Alexandre-Desplat/11vfWNvOrsZiQaCT9409q5); [Musicstax](https://musicstax.com/track/mr-moustafa/1uNhvxpkPrNHUNfeclo6xD) (search summary; pages returned 403)

**Fantastic Mr. Fox (2009)**
- Instruments: banjos, guitars, fiddles, glockenspiels and unusual percussion rather than a symphony orchestra. Desplat whistles in "Bean's Secret Cider Cellar" — [Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2009/11/13/fantastic-mr-fox-alexandre-desplat/)
- "Mr. Fox in the Fields": bouncy country rhythm of picked banjos, pizzicato strings and glockenspiels under a cello melody. "Boggis, Bunce and Bean": a "pompous, self-important march". "Whack-Bat Majorette": "a perfect pastiche of a John Philip Sousa march played by a high school football band". "Bean's Secret Cider Cellar": bold snare and timpani, twanging Jew's harp, acoustic guitars, fluttering bass flutes. The reviewer names Morricone's westerns as the model — [Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2009/11/13/fantastic-mr-fox-alexandre-desplat/)

**Moonrise Kingdom (2012)**
- "The Heroic Weather-Conditions of the Universe" is modelled on Britten's *Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra*. It is an additive, rondo-like march that starts small and brings in harps, organ, glockenspiels and other instruments one by one — [HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/moonrise-kingdom-soundtrack-wes-anderson-desplat_n_1533430); [MetaFilter](https://www.metafilter.com/118417/Guides-to-the-Orchestra-Britten-and-Moonrise-Kingdom) (search summaries)
- Desplat: "For me, comic scores always have to have some melancholy in the background." — [Wikipedia: Moonrise Kingdom (score)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonrise_Kingdom_(score))

**Isle of Dogs (2018)**
- The taiko drums carry the boy's melodic motif. Around them: saxophones, recorders, French horns, double bass and a chanting male choir. The only Japanese instrument is the taiko — [Gold Derby](https://www.goldderby.com/article/2018/alexandre-desplat-composer-isle-of-dogs-wes-anderson-animated-feature-video-interview-news/); [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2019/01/alexandre-desplat-isle-of-dogs-wes-anderson-composer-interview-1202520992/) (search summaries)
- Desplat avoided "a big symphony score and anything too sentimental" in favour of "Japanese minimalism". He also said "As the film becomes more dangerous, so does the score" — [Awards Daily](https://www.awardsdaily.com/2019/01/04/alexandre-desplat-on-scoring-isle-of-dogs/)

**The French Dispatch (2021)**
- Solo piano (Jean-Yves Thibaudet) is "the bones", and single instruments are added as "flesh": harpsichord, bassoon, tuba, percussion, banjo — [Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/features/banjos-and-bassoons-how-the-french-dispatch-composer-alexandre-desplat-collaborates-with-wes-anderson/5166477.article). Mandolin is also used, and a tuba opening evokes New Orleans jazz — [The Credits / MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2021/11/how-composer-alexandre-desplat-put-a-dada-istic-spin-on-the-french-dispatch/)
- Desplat: "I like to use just one of each instrument. I don't like to lose the melody in a large ensemble." — [Variety / search summary](https://variety.com/2021/artisans/news/french-dispatch-score-alexandre-desplat-randall-poster-1235094565/)
- The music had to be "sparse and minimal, to leave room for what's on screen... It's the opposite of what you would think — that the score should be big and active, mirroring what's on screen." — [Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/features/banjos-and-bassoons-how-the-french-dispatch-composer-alexandre-desplat-collaborates-with-wes-anderson/5166477.article)
- "Police Cooking" is built on three bass notes repeated. Desplat: "if I had written a crazy full-speed bombastic score, there'd be no way you would be able to digest what's going on." — [The Credits / MPA](https://www.motionpictures.org/2021/11/how-composer-alexandre-desplat-put-a-dada-istic-spin-on-the-french-dispatch/)
- Working method: Desplat offers "10, 20, 30 motifs or instrumental colours", and Anderson picks and rearranges them — [Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/features/banjos-and-bassoons-how-the-french-dispatch-composer-alexandre-desplat-collaborates-with-wes-anderson/5166477.article)

**Asteroid City (2023)**
- The score sits beside 1950s country, skiffle and bluegrass. Jean-Yves Lozac'h plays banjo on score tracks. One reviewer found Desplat's leitmotif "gets a little boring" over repeated hearings — [The Playlist](https://theplaylist.net/wes-andersons-asteroid-city-soundtrack-features-jarvis-cocker-alexandre-desplat-bing-crosby-more-20230620/) (search summary)

**Mark Mothersbaugh (Bottle Rocket, Rushmore, Tenenbaums, Life Aquatic)**
- From *Bottle Rocket* on, Anderson "didn't like bass sounds. He didn't like brass. He liked plucky instruments and bells and flutes and piccolos." — [Little White Lies](https://lwlies.com/articles/mark-mothersbaugh-on-working-with-wes-anderson/)
- Harpsichords and clavichords were auditioned at a rental shop on Larchmont Boulevard. For *Life Aquatic*, Mothersbaugh used an old Oberheim ("TVS1") with an **eight-step sequencer**. Music was recorded "six to eight people at a time" — [Little White Lies](https://lwlies.com/articles/mark-mothersbaugh-on-working-with-wes-anderson/)
- *Rushmore* used glockenspiel, harpsichord and flute and was "more Vivaldi influenced with a lot more sixteenth notes". *Tenenbaums* uses "a lot of harp... and a lot of harpsichord" — [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/tv-movies/tv-movie-news/devos-mothersbaugh-talks-tenenbaums-72385/) (search summary; page redirected)

### Inferences
- Inference: the reusable Anderson recipe is (a) a steady subdivided plucked ostinato (balalaika tremolo, banjo roll, harpsichord sixteenths or pizzicato), (b) one clear treble melody on a struck or plucked colour (cimbalom, glockenspiel, celesta, whistle), (c) little or no bass and brass, (d) a thin texture with one player per part, (e) a wistful minor tinge under a major key ("melancholy in the background").
- Inference: "Deadpan" comes from rigid tempo and the music refusing to hit gags. "Whimsy" comes from timbre: folk, toy or antique instruments. For this game, that means precise, unreactive tempo and odd but official-sounding colours.
- Inference: the Britten or Moonrise additive build (one instrument added per section) maps well to a desk game. Layers can be added as the rulebook grows each day, and the loop keeps changing without the tempo changing.
- Inference: Mr. Moustafa at 150 (felt as 75) suggests a waltz-like or two-feel lilt. A brisk 2/4 in the 100–120 BPM range with sixteenth-note plucks gives the Anderson "clockwork" without a march's stomp.

### Gaps
- No verified BPM for "Mr. Fox in the Fields", "Night Train to Nebelsbad", "Police Cooking" or the Moonrise suite (tempo sites blocked or silent).
- No primary Desplat quote found that uses the words "clockwork" or "metronome". "Metronomically precise" is a critic's phrase.
- Mothersbaugh's harpsichord and "sixteenth notes" details come from a search summary of a Rolling Stone piece that could not be fetched.

## 2. Theodore Shapiro's *Severance*: instruments, harmony, the unsettling-corporate quality

### Takeaway
*Severance* uses a solo piano on a four-chord minor loop, slow and sparse, and makes it strange by digital processing (reversed, chopped, glitching piano; percussion sampled from a struck piano) rather than by adding instruments. The office feel comes from clean, repetitive, emotionally withheld music that slowly "frays".

### Cited Findings
- The score started out more electronic and beat-driven. After a meeting with Ben Stiller, Shapiro played a chord progression Stiller had singled out on the piano and moved to a piano-centred approach — [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2025/05/severance-composer-theodore-shapiro-interview-1236390213/) (search summary)
- The main theme is built on **four chords**. Helly's theme uses a different four-chord progression as a B section. The "radio static" is **a reversed piano note chopped into glitchy, stuttering chunks** — [SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/826702/severance-composer-on-how-that-eerie-theme-came-together-and-the-easter-egg-you-probably-didnt-catch-interview/)
- Shapiro: "I wanted to play around with the idea of that piano glitching and falling apart", i.e. the piano stands in for the character's fraying self. Distortion layers build across the season, and the finale adds a "throbbing, percussive but subtle underbelly" — [SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/826702/severance-composer-on-how-that-eerie-theme-came-together-and-the-easter-egg-you-probably-didnt-catch-interview/)
- In season 2 many sounds use only the piano as a source, including percussion made by beating the piano and looping it. Prepared-piano sounds are often mistaken for synths — [Go See Talk](https://goseetalk.com/interview-theodore-shapiro-breaks-down-the-music-and-method-of-severance-season-2/); [Gold Derby](https://www.goldderby.com/feature/theodore-shapiro-severance-composer-video-interview-1204975163/) (search summaries)
- Headline of Shapiro's Gold Derby interview: "a few notes on the piano" can be "scarier than a 90-piece orchestra" — [Gold Derby](https://www.goldderby.com/feature/theodore-shapiro-severance-composer-video-interview-1204975163/)
- The score is primarily piano and strings, and the static is a "stuttering, distorted radio-frequency noise" made from a reversed piano note — [Wikipedia: Music of Severance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Severance)
- Tempo and key of "Main Titles": **C minor at 64 BPM** — [Tunebat](https://tunebat.com/Info/Main-Titles-Theodore-Shapiro/2dssT0G1eXmqLoNIzKEuxN) (search summary). The same search summary gave the chords as **C minor, G♭ major, F major, A♭ minor** (source page unclear; not verified). **C minor at 128 BPM** — [HDpiano](https://hdpiano.com/lesson/severance-main-title-theme-by-theodore-shapiro). 64 and 128 are a half/double-time reading of the same pulse.
- Shapiro has scored about 50 comedies, "most of them with a straight face" — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/486060013) (search summary; page timed out)

### Inferences
- Inference: the "corporate" effect comes from repetition plus restraint. A short chord loop that never resolves or develops sounds like a routine. Processing the one instrument (stutter, reverse, detune) sounds like the institution malfunctioning politely.
- Inference: if the reported chords are right (i–♭V–IV–♭vii-ish around C minor), the tritone move C→G♭ gives the uncanny tilt. For an *affectionate* satire, the same trick could sit in a major or Dorian frame with one "wrong" chromatic chord, so the game stays unsettling without turning into horror.
- Inference: the game's "stress" could be scored the Severance way. As the shift clock runs down, add stutter or bit-crush to the same piano loop instead of adding instruments or speeding up.

### Gaps
- No primary source confirms the chord spellings. The 64 vs 128 BPM conflict comes from different tempo readings, not a real disagreement.
- The Hollywood Reporter and Gold Derby interviews redirected to a paywall, so Shapiro's own words on how the music evokes office life are only partly captured.

## 3. Office and bureaucracy satire: Yes Minister, The Office (UK), Brazil, Kafka, Tati, Monty Python, The Death of Stalin

### Takeaway
Bureaucracy satire tends to borrow the institution's own sounds and ceremony: Westminster chimes (Yes Minister), a military-band march (Python), typewriters as rhythm (Brazil), a building's hum (Tati), official Soviet symphonic style (Death of Stalin). It plays that material completely straight, and the comedy comes from pomp, cheer or dread applied to trivial paperwork.

### Cited Findings
**Yes Minister / Yes, Prime Minister (Ronnie Hazlehurst)**
- The theme is "largely based on the chimes (though with a longer duration for the first note of each quarter...)". Hazlehurst: "That's all it is. It's the easiest thing I've ever done." — [Wikipedia: Westminster Quarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Quarters)
- The Westminster Quarters are five permutations of four bells in **E major**: G♯4 F♯4 E4 B3 / E4 G♯4 F♯4 B3 / E4 F♯4 G♯4 E4 / G♯4 E4 F♯4 B3 / B3 F♯4 G♯4 E4. Each change is **three quarter notes then a half note** — [Wikipedia: Westminster Quarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Quarters)

**The Office (UK)**
- The theme is "Handbags and Gladrags" (Mike d'Abo, 1960s), arranged by Big George in 2000. It comes as a short instrumental for the opening, a short vocal (Fin Muir) for the closing, and one episode has a David Brent version — [Wikipedia: Handbags and Gladrags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handbags_and_Gladrags)

**Brazil (1985, Michael Kamen)**
- Kamen builds the score from Ary Barroso's 1939 samba "Aquarela do Brasil". It opens with an extended **whistled** version. "Central Services (The Office)" uses **typewriters as the rhythmic foundation**. "The Elevator" is "humorously accelerating". Woodblocks and subtle synths add colour, and there are prominent flute and clarinet solos — [Filmtracks](https://www.filmtracks.com/titles/brazil.html)
- The office cue is "a fast-paced, energetic, and uplifting number" that "encapsulates the madness and stupidity of the bureaucracy", ending with "a little swirl of Aquarela do Brasil" — [Professional Moron](https://professionalmoron.com/2018/01/26/in-praise-of-brazils-energetic-office-theme-music/)

**Jacques Tati, *Playtime* (1967, music Francis Lemarque)**
- Tati "used sound rather than visual cues to direct the audience's attention". Dialogue is "frequently reduced to the level of background noise". Leonard Maltin: Tati was the "only man in movie history to get a laugh out of the hum of a neon sign!" — [Wikipedia: Playtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playtime)
- The interiors have "a constant electrical buzz" and a "hyper-accentuated noise of a belching chair". The overall effect is "ubiquitous faulty gadgets" — [Media Factory sound study](https://www.mediafactory.org.au/daniel-bowden/2017/03/23/a-sound-study-of-jacques-tatis-playtime/)
- Lemarque's opening-credits music is described as "exhilarating, percussive, jazzy" — (search summary; source page among [Moviejawn](https://www.moviejawn.com/home/2026/7/21/disc-dispatch-playtime) / [ART & Thoughts](https://artandthoughtz.wordpress.com/2014/07/11/playtime-1967-directed-by-jacques-tati/), not verified)

**Monty Python's Flying Circus**
- The theme is Sousa's "The Liberty Bell" (1893, **F major**, extended trio form), recorded by the Band of the Grenadier Guards. Gilliam chose it because it was public domain and seemed incongruous, and said the opening bell strike gave an impression of "getting straight down to business". The TV edit starts with one bell strike, played louder — [Wikipedia: The Liberty Bell (march)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Liberty_Bell_(march))
- Conflict: Wikipedia dates the recording to 1967. [Songfacts](https://calendar.songfacts.com/october/5/21489) (search summary) says 1938.

**The Death of Stalin (2017, Christopher Willis)**
- Willis borrowed a Shostakovich trait: "a sort of fast, intense style of music... but it refuses to stay happy and upbeat for long, and then becomes tortured and manic." He used an enlarged orchestra with extra woodwinds and big strings, processed to sound like a Stalin-era concert-hall recording — [Classical Music](https://www.classical-music.com/features/artists/composer-chris-willis-discusses-his-soundtrack-death-stalin)
- Willis: early talks with Iannucci were about "the overall tempo of the film and a certain nervousness that there should be ticking away in the background". Also: "a more sober film would score those scenes with nothing at all. I felt like it suited the film much better if the score was mad and full-on." — [Substream](https://substreammagazine.com/2018/06/interview-composer-christopher-willis-death-of-stalin/)
- Willis on the goal: "give you the nervousness of the film and genuine danger, but also not tap into a straightforward drama". Influences: Shostakovich, Prokofiev, Weinberg — [Alex Moreland interview](https://alexmoreland.co.uk/2017/10/19/interview-christopher-willis-the-death-of-stalin/)
- A reviewer says the score "ignores that *The Death of Stalin* is a comedy and instead unfailingly maintains its serious, severe nature" — [The Film Scorer](https://thefilmscorer.com/the-death-of-stalin-christopher-willis-2018/)

**Kafka adaptations**
- Soderbergh's *Kafka* (1991) is scored by Cliff Martinez — [Wikipedia: Kafka (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafka_(film)). Search summaries variously credit a zither, cimbalom or hammered dulcimer (with violin and accordion) and a *Third Man* influence, but no fetched page confirmed the instrument (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- Inference: the Yes Minister trick is directly usable. A four-note civic chime (E–major bell pitches, 3 quarters + half note) works as the Ministry's "logo" and as shift-quarter time signals, and it is funny because it is literally just the clock.
- Inference: Brazil's typewriter rhythm and Tati's neon hum support building the game's percussion from its own stamp thunk, paper shuffle and fluorescent-light buzz. This fits the "never winks" rule, because the Ministry's own machinery is making the music.
- Inference: Death of Stalin shows that a *straight*, nervous and slightly manic score can be funnier than a comic one. Willis's "nervousness... ticking away in the background" is a close model for a shift clock. The warning is that a full-on Soviet march played continuously would repeat the "grating march" problem, so it belongs in short stingers or crisis moments.
- Inference: the Python/Liberty Bell lesson is that the pompous march works as a **short, bookending theme** (title, day start), not as a loop under concentrated work.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable instrumentation or tempo breakdown for the Yes Minister arrangement, the Office UK instrumental opening, or Lemarque's *Playtime* office cues.
- Kafka (1991) instrumentation is unconfirmed. Snippets conflict between zither, cimbalom and hammered dulcimer, and Discogs credits were blocked.
- No BPMs were found for Brazil's "Central Services" or for the Liberty Bell recording.

## 4. Hold music, Muzak and library music as satire: what makes it recognisable

### Takeaway
Institutional background music is recognisable by soft instrumental arrangements with no vocals ("shimmering strings" with piano, guitar or horns; bossa nova or lounge; later FM-synth new age), flattened dynamics, short loops, and function over expression. Muzak openly designed it to raise office productivity in timed blocks, which makes it a good diegetic joke for a Ministry.

### Cited Findings
- Muzak used "soft, melodic instrumental versions of popular tunes, usually with shimmering strings, along with pianos, guitars, or horns", with no vocals because "lyrics might knock a worker or a patron out of the rhythm" — [Red Bull Music Academy](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2012/09/history-of-muzak/)
- **Stimulus Progression** (trademarked in the 1940s): music "programmed in 15-minute blocks, gradually getting faster in tempo and louder and brassier in instrumentation, to encourage workers to speed up their pace". After each block, "the music would fall silent for 15 minutes", which company research said "limited listener fatigue" — [Wikipedia: Muzak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzak)
- Later Muzak versions replaced vocals "by either a piano, woodwind, or guitar". Ted Nugent (1986): Muzak causes "uncontrollable fits of blandness" — [Wikipedia: Muzak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzak)
- Eno defined ambient against Muzak: Muzak's "intention is to 'brighten' the environment by adding stimulus to it", whereas ambient should "accommodate many levels of listening attention" — [Red Bull Music Academy](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2012/09/history-of-muzak/)
- Elevator music: "soft" pop or "light" classical "performed by slow strings" (Mantovani, Franck Pourcel, James Last). It removes dynamic extremes and spans "easy listening, lounge, piano solo, jazz, bossa nova or middle of the road". Comic use: intense scenes "interrupted or interspersed with such anodyne music while characters use an elevator". Game examples: *Metal Gear Solid 4*, *System Shock*, *GoldenEye 007* — [Wikipedia: Elevator music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator_music)
- Hold music: Alfred Levy's "telephone hold program system" patent (filed 1962, granted 1966). Early endless-loop cartridges would "stretch, warp and disintegrate, producing ominous, warbling audio". Many systems looped only a few minutes of content. Hold music is "the soundtrack of modern bureaucracy" — [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/a-brief-history-of-hold-music-from-early-patents-to-the-soundtrack-of-customer-service-purgatory-180989185/)
- Cisco's "Opus No. 1" (Tim Carleton, Darrick Deel, 1989) was recorded on a **Yamaha DX7** onto a four-track Tascam Portastudio and was "inspired from Yanni, Kitaro and Art of Noise". It is used by Centrelink, the NHS and the RCMP — [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/a-brief-history-of-hold-music-from-early-patents-to-the-soundtrack-of-customer-service-purgatory-180989185/). It was played live on a DX7IIFD through an Alesis Midiverb — [Wikipedia: Music on hold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_on_hold) (search summary)

### Inferences
- Inference: the recognisable ingredients to synthesise are an FM electric piano or "glass" bell patch (DX7-style), a soft bossa-nova guitar/rim-click pattern, pad strings, no lead vocal, compressed dynamics, a narrow band-passed "telephone" EQ (roughly 300–3400 Hz, the standard telephone voice band; not sourced here), and a short obvious loop. Major-seventh and ninth chords are the stereotypical easy-listening harmony. This is common knowledge, but I found no source to cite for it.
- Inference: Stimulus Progression gives a ready-made game mechanic that stays in character. The Ministry's piped music gets slightly faster and brassier across a day or shift, then stops for a stretch. The silence gaps also answer the "looping music grates" problem, and the fatigue research is cited above.
- Inference: warped or wow-and-flutter tape (the dying loop cartridge) is a deadpan way to show a failing institution or a stressed player, without adding a comic instrument.

### Gaps
- No verified BPM for "Opus No. 1" or for typical Muzak Stimulus Progression tempi. The actual "stimulus value" scale Muzak used was not found.
- No source was found for comedic *film/TV* uses of hold music beyond the generic elevator-scene trope.

## 5. Comedic underscoring conventions: bassoon, tuba, pizzicato, woodblock, stingers; wacky (Mickey-Mousing) versus deadpan (playing it straight)

### Takeaway
The default "comedy" palette (sneaky pizzicato, bassoon, tuba, woodblock, synced stingers) is a stock-library and cartoon convention, and critics have long called close sync crude. The stronger comedy tradition, from Bernstein's *Airplane!* through Shapiro and Willis, plays the scene straight or against it, so the music believes the absurd situation. That approach fits a game that "never winks".

### Cited Findings
- Mickey Mousing "syncs the accompanying music with the actions on screen". It dates to *Steamboat Willie* (1928). Cocteau called it "the most vulgar technique used in film music", Eisler "This awful Wagnerian illustration technique!", and it "is somewhat out of favor today... because of overuse". It "may create unintentional humor" or be used for parody — [Wikipedia: Mickey Mousing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mickey_Mousing)
- Pizzicato is "especially popular in comedic music for its light, staccato sound", often for tiptoeing or sneaky behaviour. The typical stock comedy orchestration is pizzicato strings, bassoon, tuba, clarinet and percussion — stock-library descriptions: [AudioJungle](https://audiojungle.net/item/comical-goofy-pizzicato/19581200); [Apple Music "Whimsical Orchestral Comedy: Pizzicato Strings, Tuba & Woodwinds (Sneaky Light Theme)"](https://music.apple.com/us/album/whimsical-orchestral-comedy-pizzicato-strings-tuba/1863981998); [DL Sounds guide](https://www.dl-sounds.com/comedy-music-in-media-a-guide-for-content-creators/) (search summaries). A practitioner forum thread is titled "Scoring comedy without pizzicato" — [VI-Control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/scoring-comedy-without-pizzicato.125361/) (page blocked; title only)
- Elmer Bernstein's *Airplane!* is "a fully orchestrated score" with "the grand, suspenseful feel of a true disaster epic" that "clashes hilariously with the visuals". - Desplat, *Isle of Dogs*: avoided "anything too sentimental". *French Dispatch*: kept the music sparse "to leave room for what's on screen". ### Inferences
- Inference: bassoon, tuba and sneaky pizzicato *are* usable if they are played "officially": legato, in strict time, as part of a civic band or chamber group (Desplat put bassoon and tuba in *French Dispatch*). They become "wacky" when they slide, honk, or sync to player actions. For this game, keep them unsynced, in time and unornamented.
- Inference: playing it straight is funnier here because the joke lives in the evidence (raccoons in a trench coat). Music that reacts would be the Ministry winking. Music that ignores the absurdity keeps the clerk's poker face and leaves the laugh to the player.
- Inference: stingers are fine where the *institution* would make a sound (stamp, bell, chime, typewriter bell, "ding" of a counter). Descending trombones, slide whistles and "wah-wah" sounds read as the game making fun of itself.
- Inference: a ticking pulse (Dunkirk, Death of Stalin's "nervousness ticking away") is a natural partner for the shift clock. It should be quiet and felt in the percussion, not melodic.

## 6. Synthesis: 3 to 5 concrete directions for this game's music

### Takeaway
Five directions, all played straight and none reacting to jokes. (A) Anderson-style "Registry Clockwork" chamber ostinato. (B) Severance-style "Ministry Piano" loop that degrades under time pressure. (C) Diegetic "Stimulus Progression" hold-music from the Ministry's own PA. (D) Westminster-style civic chimes and a band bookend as stingers only. (E) Built-in desk percussion from the game's own SFX. A likely combination is B or A for the shift bed, C for waiting or menu screens, D for day start/end, and E layered under whichever bed is playing. Everything in the "Directions" table is **inference** built on the cited traits above. Tempo ranges are proposals unless a reference BPM is cited.

**Cross-cutting rules (inference)**
- Do not sync music to accept/challenge outcomes or to applicant absurdity. That would be Mickey-Mousing and break "never winks" and the design invariant that only evidence reveals validity. Any audio sting must fire identically for valid and invalid outcomes, or only on neutral events (stamp, bell, clock).
- To fix "too sleepy": raise the pulse (plucked 8ths/16ths at 104–120 instead of pads), keep dynamics flat. To fix "march grated": avoid 2/4 oom-pah with snare rolls under the work loop. Use the Anderson plucked ostinato instead of a band, rotate melodic variants, and borrow Muzak's silence gaps.
- Let the melody stay singable and slightly melancholy (Desplat), with one of each instrument so it cuts through desk SFX.

### Gaps
- None of the proposed tempo ranges are validated by player testing. The 104–120 BPM range for A extrapolates from Mr. Moustafa's reported 150/75 BPM and general practice.
- Easy-listening harmony (major 7ths, bossa patterns) and the telephone-band EQ figure are standard practice but uncited here.
- The legal status of quoting the Westminster Quarters melody verbatim in a game was not researched. It is a 1793-era chime, very likely public domain, but that is unverified.
