# Comedy delivery in acclaimed games: timing, repetition and tone (for a deadpan satirical desk game)

Method note: quotes below were pulled from the linked pages with a summarizing fetch tool and asked for verbatim. They are very likely accurate but should be checked against the original page before being quoted in anything published. Items drawn only from a search-result summary (not read on the page) are marked "(search summary, not verified on page)". Steam and forum posts are marked "(forum)".

## 1. How the named games deliver jokes, avoid repetition, and time jokes against gameplay

### Takeaway
The games that stay funny give jokes a place outside the moments that need the player's full attention. They put lines at the start or end of a task, or in downtime used as a reward. The player's own action pulls the trigger (Goose), and the lines stay short. They avoid repetition with memory ("said once"), cooldowns between beats of a running gag, context-specific lines that take priority over generic ones, and generic filler lines written to be forgettable. Running gags work when each return escalates inside the rules. Papers, Please's Jorji fails a different, newer rule on each visit.

### Cited Findings

**Timing against gameplay (Valve / Portal)**
- Valve on GLaDOS placement: "We almost always have GLaDOS talk at the beginning and end of a puzzle. Portal 1 playtesting showed us pretty quickly that even though people like hearing GLaDOS, they really, really don't want to listen to her while they're actively thinking through a puzzle." Also: GLaDOS is typically added "at the tail end of a map's production." — [Game Informer, "Thinking With Portals: Making A Portal 2 Test Chamber"](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber.aspx)
- Portal 2 developer commentary ("Horrible Accents", Erik Robson): the breakout sequence "was originally a lot longer, involving Wheatley talking to you in a horrible American accent." Playtests showed "every player made a beeline for the opening," so they had to "ditch all the dialogue." — [Portal Wiki transcript of Portal 2 developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_2_developer_commentary)
- Portal 2 commentary ("Elevating Videos", Ido Magal): elevator videos became "a means of relaying information about the larger world of Aperture" while also serving "as a reward for completing test chambers." — [Portal Wiki, Portal 2 developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_2_developer_commentary)
- Portal 2 commentary ("Writing for Co-Op", Chet Faliszek): players talk over GLaDOS in co-op, so the team "broke up the story beats into smaller sections" and "repeat the points multiple times to insure the message sinks in." — [Portal Wiki, Portal 2 developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_2_developer_commentary)
- Jay Pinkerton on length: "the guy playing this game is not going to sit there for 10 minutes and listen to this and think it's as funny as we think it is." — [Game Informer, "Writer's Block: Portal 2 Writers Roundtable"](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/12/feature-writers-block-valve-writers-talk-portal-2.aspx)
- Portal 1 commentary (Chet Faliszek) on the Companion Cube: "Once the dialog went in, playtesters went from routinely abandoning the box to never wanting it to leave their side." Also on turrets: "robots can never really be cute until they are talking robots." — [Portal Wiki, Portal developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_developer_commentary)

**Avoiding repetition: bark systems (Valve, Supergiant)**
- Valve's response-rules system (Elan Ruskin, GDC 2012, used in Left 4 Dead, Portal, the Orange Box and Dota): "Rules can also write context back to the character or the world. This context is appended to the next query performed by the character. Creates memory, the ability to pick subsequent lines based on what happened before." — [Ruskin, "AI-driven Dynamic Dialog through Fuzzy Pattern Matching" (slides text)](https://archive.org/stream/valve-publications/2012/GDC2012_Ruskin_Elan_DynamicDialog_djvu.txt); [slides PDF](https://cdn.akamai.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2012/GDC2012_Ruskin_Elan_DynamicDialog.pdf)
- Same talk, on cooldowns for running gags: "you can have automatic expiration times on a particular fact, if you want to prevent two successive bits of a running gag from being played too close together." — [Ruskin GDC 2012 slides text](https://archive.org/stream/valve-publications/2012/GDC2012_Ruskin_Elan_DynamicDialog_djvu.txt)
- Same talk, on specificity: the system is "A system of general rules superceded by exceptions for particular circumstances," meaning the most specific matching line wins over the generic one. — [Ruskin GDC 2012 slides text](https://archive.org/stream/valve-publications/2012/GDC2012_Ruskin_Elan_DynamicDialog_djvu.txt)
- Greg Kasavin (Hades) on repeatable lines: "before any of it repeats, you would have to have looped through the game like 20 or 30 times, so hours and hours of play before a small line will repeat, for example." And: "you probably won't remember it anyway, because it's not a line that should feel kind of repetitive if you were to hear it again. That's how we designed the narrative of the game." — [GeekDad interview with Greg Kasavin (2019)](https://geekdad.com/2019/10/narrative-and-early-access-supergiants-greg-kasavin-discusses-hades-development/)

**Systemic and reactive delivery (Untitled Goose Game)**
- The core list was "mostly a bunch of slapstick cliches, things like 'Steal someone's glasses, they grope around on the ground for them'." — [Game Developer, "Road to the IGF: House House's Untitled Goose Game"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-house-house-s-i-untitled-goose-game-i-)
- On timing: "It was funnier when the goose tried to steal things when people weren't looking than it was for the goose to just run up and take things." They used stealth tropes: "very limited vision" and "very discrete AI states for when characters had noticed something missing or had spotted the goose." — [Game Developer, Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-house-house-s-i-untitled-goose-game-i-)
- On the player as performer: controlling the goose "felt performative; as though the player was controlling a puppet for an audience." — [Game Developer, Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-house-house-s-i-untitled-goose-game-i-)
- Critic Jini Maxwell: the to-do list "offers players opportunities to be the comedian – setting up the punchline, and then letting you make the joke"; "the joy comes from anticipation – you understand immediately what is about to happen"; the game is "mercifully restrained" (contrasted with High on Life's rapid-fire dialogue); "By positioning the player as the comedian, it bypasses the common pitfalls of comedy in games." — [ACMI, "Untitled Goose Game's player-comedians"](https://www.acmi.net.au/stories-and-ideas/untitled-goose-player-comedians/)
- House House's comedic influences ran "from Postman Pat to Buster Keaton." — [ACMI collection entry](https://www.acmi.net.au/works/121785--untitled-goose-game/) (search summary, not verified on page)

**Running gags that escalate through the rules (Papers, Please)**
- Jorji Costava: day 3 arrives with no passport ("Arstotzka so great, passport not required"); day 4 brings a crude fake passport from a nonexistent country; day 6 has a valid passport but no entry permit; day 8 still has no permit and presents an entry ticket, which was only valid on day 3. He stays upbeat however often he is rejected or arrested. — [Papers Please Wiki, Jorji Costava](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Jorji_Costava) (search summary; direct fetch blocked)
- The rules grow through daily bulletins: "specific regulations and procedures are frequently updated via daily bulletins distributed to the inspector," e.g. entry tickets (Day 3) and identity supplements (Day 13). — [Morrissette, "Glory to Arstotzka", Game Studies 17(1)](https://gamestudies.org/1701/articles/morrissette)
- Lucas Pope on levity: "for levity I tried to include more lighthearted encounters and individuals; something like what I imagine real inspectors deal with." He "developed the game from the bottom up. Only once I had the core mechanics of document checking working well did I start to think about a narrative to tie everything together." — [Reason interview with Lucas Pope (2013)](https://reason.com/2013/09/26/papers-please-politics-in-games-and-the/)

**Environmental density, callbacks and structure (Thank Goodness You're Here!)**
- VGC (Jordan Middler, 5/5): "There are more brilliant jokes in the first five minutes of the game than most games with 'funny dialogue' get in a full runtime." "Each time you return to a part of the town you've already visited, there will be a new joke, a new side quest, or a new route to another new area." "later jokes in the game land so much better when you've received the earlier setup." "There are dozens and dozens of jokes that we entirely missed the first time through an area" (e.g. a freezer thermostat reading "37C"). "Like any great comedy, Thank Goodness You're Here doesn't outstay its welcome" (about three hours). — [VGC review](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/review/thank-goodness-youre-here/)
- Coal Supper (the developers) moved from an open world to a linear town because "people were often in the wrong areas, just doing laps through pathways and missing the one thing they were supposed to do." — [Game Developer, "'Humor is all we've really got'"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-humor-is-all-we-ve-really-got-championing-absurdity-in-thank-goodness-you-re-here-)
- Coal Supper: "We find that there's a sweet spot for visual information, wherein it's just about too much to process, leaving you to spot things in the noise." — [Thumbsticks, "How Thank Goodness You're Here! puts comedy first"](https://www.thumbsticks.com/how-thank-goodness-youre-here-puts-comedy-first/)

**Line length and rhythm (Night in the Woods)**
- Scott Benson: "I basically wrote everything in tweet length." "You sort of learn how to segment longer thoughts, add pauses and single word tweets for humor and tension, all that kind of stuff." "Chopping it up into smaller bits let us play with rhythm and pauses and personality and so forth." "There's nothing worse that opening up a conversation in-game and it's just like paragraphs of unbroken text on screen." — [Cartridge Lit interview with Scott Benson (2018)](https://cartridgelit.com/2018/05/15/interview-scott-benson/)

**Surprise and discovery (Frog Fractions, The Stanley Parable)**
- Jim Crawford: "Stumbling on an unexpected secret makes the world feel more like a real space, and less like a crafted amusement park ride." Frog Fractions hid "increasingly bizarre" genre-scrambling minigames under what looked like educational software. — [Game Developer, "Frog Fractions creator finds value in keeping secrets"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-i-frog-fractions-i-creator-finds-value-in-keeping-secrets)
- Davey Wreden: "The very first thing I asked with the game was 'what would happen if you could disobey the narrator?'" — [Shacknews interview with Davey Wreden](https://www.shacknews.com/article/70363/interview-davey-wreden-on-stanley-parable-remake-and-self-taught)
- In Stanley Parable: Ultra Deluxe, each ending returns you to the same office with the same opening lines while the Narrator grows more self-aware across loops. One new "ending" is jumping in a circle 36 times, after which the Narrator quits in disgust. Reviewers also complained the Narrator sometimes talks on while the player waits, and wanted a skip button (the game makes a gag of one). — [VideoChums review](https://videochums.com/review/the-stanley-parable-ultra-deluxe); [CogConnected review](https://cogconnected.com/review/the-stanley-parable-ultra-deluxe-review/) (search summary, not verified on page)

**Recurring voices (Cave Johnson)**
- Erik Wolpaw created Cave Johnson as an "eccentric dead billionaire," an "industrial, Southern guy" set against Aperture's "anti-septic" corporate nature. His lines are all pre-recorded messages the player hears in the order the company declined. GameSpy's Will Tuttle praised J.K. Simmons for his ability to "adeptly blend gravitas and silliness." — [Wikipedia, Cave Johnson (Portal)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave_Johnson_(Portal)) (citing National Post, GameSpy)
- Wolpaw on the Portal 2 personality spheres: "you can do a lot of bits with them without making a ton of work for somebody else." — [Game Informer Portal 2 writers roundtable](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/12/feature-writers-block-valve-writers-talk-portal-2.aspx)

### Inferences
- The window remark follows Valve's GLaDOS rule. It lands best when the applicant steps up (before the player starts checking) or right after the stamp, never as text the player must read while cross-checking documents. Longer material (Gazette, court closing line, bills) is Portal 2's "elevator video": a reward in a stretch with no clock.
- The three repetition tools map onto the game's pools. Valve's memory is "said once", which the game already does. Valve's cooldown means two beats of the same running gag never land on the same day. Valve's specificity means a line tied to today's rule or this applicant's papers beats a generic one. Hades adds a fourth: if a pool can run dry, the fallback lines should be written to be forgettable. Recycling a vivid line is the worse option.
- Jorji is the closest precedent for a recurring applicant in a rulebook game. The escalation engine is the rulebook itself: each time he comes back he is caught (or nearly caught) by the newest rule, and his mood never changes. This matches the project's plan for Pat's attempts, and it keeps each gag tied to a clue on screen.
- Thank Goodness You're Here!'s density works because jokes sit in the background and the player finds them. That supports putting the high-density material in posters, bill items and the Gazette, which the player can read at leisure, and keeping the foreground (the window) sparse during the shift.
- The move away from an open world ("missing the one thing they were supposed to do") argues for making sure a set-piece applicant is actually reached. A set piece placed in the first half of the queue, as `notes/game-design.md` already says, protects it from a player who runs out of time.

## 2. What writers and designers say, and what critics say landed or fell flat

### Takeaway
The makers agree on a few things. Say less, and cut the setup ("chop off the beginning and the end"). Don't be funny all the time. Plan running jokes "along the spine" of the game. Keep the tone in line with the play experience so failure isn't mocked. Accept that some jokes will miss, and test with fresh eyes, because the writers stopped laughing long ago. Critics praise jokes the player discovers and setups that pay off later. They punish nonstop chatter, lines that contradict what just happened, meta-jokes the game hasn't earned, and borrowed or dated references.

### Cited Findings

**Designers and writers**
- Tim Schafer (GDC 2010 panel with Rhianna Pratchett and Sean Vanaman): "You don't want to be the guy who is always funny, because he gets annoying after a while. You want to be the quiet guy who surprises everyone with a rare gem of a joke." Also: "It takes pressure off you when the player does something silly on purpose." The panel said recurring jokes "have to be very well planned" and written "along the spine of a game," and "All three panelists agreed that timing and context are key." — [Destructoid, "GDC 10: Make 'em laugh: Comedy in games"](https://www.destructoid.com/gdc-10-make-em-laugh-comedy-in-games/)
- Zach Johnson (West of Loathing): "Literally every piece of text becomes an opportunity for us to at least try to be funny." Advice as summarized in the article: treat the narrator as a character, avoid repetition as much as possible, give each NPC a distinct voice and mood, and avoid topical references that date the game or jokes borrowed from other games ("The cake joke was funny once: when Portal came out."). He chose not to punch down or make light of things people didn't choose. — [Game Developer, "Writing comedic games the West of Loathing way"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/writing-comedic-games-the-i-west-of-loathing-i-way)
- Coal Supper's named comedy principles: "Humble Pie" (dialogue, animations or other elements "should fit naturally within the game world, rather than being forced to dominate the player's attention"), "Farce as an Engine" ("Increasingly absurd but semiotically coherent narrative beats!") and "Don't Milk It" (the exact heading in the article). On editing: "If you're not sure a joke is working, try chopping off the beginning and the end." On structure: "Only when we'd killed that last and dearest of our darlings, the open world, could we see for the first time a manageable shape to this problem." — [Thumbsticks](https://www.thumbsticks.com/how-thank-goodness-youre-here-puts-comedy-first/)
- Coal Supper on testing: "It's very difficult to [test comedy] because it's a joke you're telling in private for four years, and then in public once." On process: "very slowly teasing gameplay and narrative out of the gags. If we were still laughing after a week we asked 'how does this fit?'" On taste: "We decided we need to stick to our guns and what we find funny. Then if it doesn't quite hit the mark and miss, then at least we haven't pandered and then missed." — [Game Developer, "'Humor is all we've really got'"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-humor-is-all-we-ve-really-got-championing-absurdity-in-thank-goodness-you-re-here-)
- Grace Bruxner (Frog Detective, two years' experience as a stand-up): "Find a genre that fits your setting, but not your tone." "When you subvert genre make sure it's still recognizable." "I also made it so that nothing dramatic ever happens in the game." "Not every joke will land and that's absolutely fine. Not every joke works on every person, but I wanted to have a high success rate as possible. I tried to get enough jokes in there were universally funny." — [Game Developer, "How The Haunted Island subverted genre for laughs"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-the-haunted-island-a-frog-detective-game-i-subverted-genre-for-laughs); [GDC Vault listing](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025651/Building-Games-Around-Humor-Lessons)
- Portal 2 commentary ("Slow Burn", Elan Ruskin): GLaDOS was originally "a lot more cutting" early on, but "playtesters revealed...it was a bit grueling," so the team "rewrote [her arc] to give her more of a slow burn towards the player." — [Portal Wiki, Portal 2 developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_2_developer_commentary)
- Portal 2 commentary ("Evil Wheatley", Noel McGinn) on keeping a comic character dangerous: "we still wanted him to be the bumbling idiot...but at the same time, there had to be some sense that he was dangerous now." — [Portal Wiki, Portal 2 developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_2_developer_commentary)
- Erik Wolpaw on the writing room: "two thirds goofing off and one third doing work...There's kind of a black art to it, keeping the energy up as long as you possibly can." Chet Faliszek on jokes tied to specifics during production: "the joke you wrote about the boat doesn't work because the boat is now a truck. Seriously, it just keeps changing." — [Game Informer Portal 2 writers roundtable](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/12/feature-writers-block-valve-writers-talk-portal-2.aspx)
- Greg Kasavin on tone: roguelike failure is "like stepping on a rake and having it smack you in the forehead." Supergiant wanted "a tone that is aligned with the play experience itself," with "a character who's self-effacing in the face of failure, but is very determined." — [GeekDad interview with Greg Kasavin](https://geekdad.com/2019/10/narrative-and-early-access-supergiants-greg-kasavin-discusses-hades-development/)
- William Pugh's GDC session description says he "hates how games do comedy," opposes "procedural humour simulators," and argues for "Writers writing jokes for other video game writers." — [GDC Vault, "How to Make Your Game Just Completely Hilarious: The Stanley Parable"](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1022057/How-to-Make-Your-Game)
- Zoe Quinn's GDC 2015 talk noted that playtesting is "really tricky when your game is full of jokes that might quickly fall flat when you play through them multiple times." — [Game Developer, "Designing funny games is no joke"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-designing-funny-games-is-no-joke-but-it-can-be-done)
- Portal 2 commentary ("Smooth Jazz", Mike Morasky): "the team discovered through playtesting that smooth jazz was funny to all ages, genders and cultures." — [Portal Wiki, Portal 2 developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_2_developer_commentary)

**Critics: what landed**
- Thank Goodness You're Here! (VGC, 5/5): setups and callbacks across return visits; "It threads the needle perfectly between subtle, witty British humour and writing the word 'nonce' on a wall." Its only caveat: "Non-UK audiences may not get some of the jokes." — [VGC review](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/review/thank-goodness-youre-here/)
- Hypnospace Outlaw (Nintendo Life, PJ O'Reilly, 9/10): "frequently hilarious stuff that's impeccably well-written," with "all-too-believable characters." The jokes arrive through the world itself: "flashing neon fonts, obnoxious music, low quality GIFs, crude animations and terrible, terrible poetry." — [Nintendo Life review](https://www.nintendolife.com/reviews/switch-eshop/hypnospace_outlaw)

**Critics and players: what fell flat**
- High on Life (Game Informer, Marcus Stewart, 5.75/10): "The humor isn't so much about setting up clever punchlines as it is about reiterating the same basic gag with a barrage of expletives." "High on Life screams at you for hours about how hilarious and messed up everything is." On a meta-joke: "Ripping on a pipe puzzle for being by-the-books without doing anything unique itself makes the joke feel hollow and hypocritical." On context-blind barks: "Sometimes their banter doesn't even make sense, such as a gun complaining about not being used in ages when I'd used them seconds ago." What did work: "I smiled at the aggressive banter of a family of loud-mouthed construction workers." — [Game Informer review](https://gameinformer.com/review/high-on-life/low-on-laughs)
- Disco Elysium (forum), original post by Tazor: "The intro was just perfect. So many stupid laugh out loud moments...But the more I play the more bored I feel. Almost if not all the humor is gone." Replies split: some said the tone deliberately shifts to somber, while others "laughed out loud like a maniac till the end." — [Steam discussion, "Does it ever get funny again?"](https://steamcommunity.com/app/632470/discussions/0/3057363335749569244/)

### Inferences
- "Chop off the beginning and the end" and Benson's tweet-length rule both favor a remark that is one sentence with the turn at the end. Schafer's "quiet guy" supports mostly plain filler applicants with rare memorable ones, which is already the pacing in `notes/game-design.md` ("mostly fill-ins, one or two cast appearances").
- GLaDOS's "slow burn" rewrite and Kasavin's "aligned with the play experience" apply to citation memos and fines. A dry memo about the rule reads as the world being absurd; a memo that jabs the player for failing reads as the game mocking them, and playtesters found an early, cutting GLaDOS "grueling". The project's "first mistake each day is a warning" fits this finding.
- Faliszek's "boat is now a truck" is a production warning: jokes tied to specific rules or items must be re-checked whenever the rulebook changes. Otherwise a line can point to a rule that no longer exists. In this game that would also break the fair-clue invariant.
- The High on Life gun that complains about not being used is the clearest example of a line contradicting state. In this game, a remark or memo that contradicts what is on screen is worse than unfunny, because the player is trained to trust the evidence. Lines should be selected from real state, which is test 1 of the project's own writing test ("It resolves").
- The Disco Elysium thread suggests players notice when the joke rate drops after a strong opening. Thank Goodness You're Here! shows the fix: hold material back so later jokes pay off earlier setups. For a seven-day week, that means reserving strong payoffs for days 4 to 7 and not spending the best material on day 1.
- Pugh's "writers writing jokes" position and Coal Supper's "tyrannical" control of dialogue both favor authored lines over procedural combination for anything meant to be funny. Procedural assembly suits fill-ins, not punchlines.

### Gaps
- No transcript of the Pugh, Quinn or Bruxner GDC talks was available; only session descriptions and summaries.
- No direct Erik Wolpaw quote was found on GLaDOS's joke construction beyond the roundtable and developer commentary.
- The PC Gamer "Great moments: Jorji Costava" article did not render, so there is no critic quote on why Jorji is funny.
- Papers, Please reviews gathered here stress bleakness over humor, e.g. Polygon's "exercise in misery" and GameSpot's "will stress you out" as quoted on [Wikipedia, Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please) (search summary, not verified on page). No critic was found analyzing its dry humor in depth.

## 3. Deadpan and satire: the straight-man world, bureaucratic humor, affectionate satire, and pitfalls

### Takeaway
There are two workable arrangements. In one, a sincere player-character faces an absurd world (Frog Detective's apologetic detective, the Papers, Please inspector facing Jorji). In the other, the player is the comedian and the world is the straight man (Goose's villagers keeping their routines). Satire stays affectionate when its targets are believable, sincere people and the system is what gets mocked (Hypnospace's users, Pope's "real inspectors"). The listed pitfalls are well sourced: dated memes, borrowed jokes, unearned meta-jokes, jokes that dominate attention, nonstop density in the foreground, and punching down.

### Cited Findings
- Stephen Totilo (Slate, 2004) argued that for game comedy "the best solution was to make the player a 'straight man'." — [Wikipedia, "Comedy in video games"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_in_video_games) (citing Slate, "Joystuck")
- Untitled Goose Game makes the villagers the straight men: their "commitment to retaining their usual routines" is what makes the disruption funny, as with NPCs in Hitman. — [ACMI, Jini Maxwell](https://www.acmi.net.au/stories-and-ideas/untitled-goose-player-comedians/)
- Frog Detective inverts the noir detective who doesn't care but is brilliant, making the lead friendly and apologetic; "nothing dramatic ever happens," and there is "no murder or grand conspiracies." — [Game Developer on Bruxner](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-the-haunted-island-a-frog-detective-game-i-subverted-genre-for-laughs)
- Jorji "remains upbeat" no matter how often he is rejected: the sincere, undeterred applicant as a running gag. — [Papers Please Wiki](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Jorji_Costava) (search summary)
- Pope based the comic applicants on what "real inspectors deal with." — [Reason](https://reason.com/2013/09/26/papers-please-politics-in-games-and-the/)
- Jay Tholen's affectionate source material for Hypnospace Outlaw was "the point at which it became just accessible enough for some grandma somewhere to create a virtual shrine to her deceased dog." Its "Shifty freeware," "useless toolbars, and adware" "were integral to my Web 1.0 experience." — [Inverse interview with Jay Tholen](https://www.inverse.com/article/22494-hynospace-outlaw-jay-tholen-interview)
- Cave Johnson works as satire of a type (the booming industrialist) through contrast with the setting ("industrial, Southern guy" against an "anti-septic" corporation) and through a performance with "gravitas and silliness." — [Wikipedia, Cave Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave_Johnson_(Portal))
- Pitfall, dated and borrowed references: Zach Johnson advises against topical references that date a game and against reusing other games' jokes ("The cake joke was funny once: when Portal came out."). — [Game Developer, West of Loathing](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/writing-comedic-games-the-i-west-of-loathing-i-way)
- Pitfall, dated memes: critics described Borderlands 3's humor as stuck in the late 2000s, when memes "lasted months rather than days." — [Game Rant, "Borderlands 3 jokes that have already aged poorly"](https://gamerant.com/borderlands-3-jokes-humor-aged-poorly-bad/) (search summary, not verified on page); players (forum) complained that the character Vaughn became a "brain dead memelord" — [Steam discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/397540/discussions/0/4289187621816250187) (search summary, forum)
- Pitfall, unearned meta: High on Life's jab at a generic pipe puzzle felt "hollow and hypocritical." — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/review/high-on-life/low-on-laughs). ### Inferences
- This game mixes the two models, and that should be deliberate. The Ministry is deadpan: it reports and never jokes. Applicants are sincere, like Jorji and the Frog Detective. The player is the comic whose stamp sets off a reaction, as in Goose. That lines up with the project's writing rules ("Applicants are sincere", "The Ministry never jokes; it reports"). The research supports keeping any character from winking at the player.
- Bureaucratic satire lands when it copies the real procedure closely. Pope's "real inspectors" and Hypnospace's "all-too-believable characters" both point that way. This supports the project's "Mild and close beats wild and far" rule and its use of real PoH and Kleros policy text.
- Crypto and internet slang is exactly the kind of topical reference Johnson and the Borderlands 3 critics warn about. The project's "no memes, no crypto slang" rule matches the evidence.
- Meta-jokes about "it's a game" or about Papers, Please itself are risky unless the game is built around them. The High on Life versus Stanley Parable contrast suggests leaving them out.

## 4. Player complaints about repeated lines ("arrow to the knee") and what fixed them

### Takeaway
The more flavorful a line, the faster it wears out on repeat: Skyrim's line was meant to be rare and "sticks out" because "it's got more flavor". The fixes in shipped games are memory and "said once" flags, cooldowns between beats of a gag, priority for the most specific context, a large pool with filler written to be forgettable (Hades: 20 to 30 runs before a repeat), and, as a last resort, a player-facing setting to reduce chatter (High on Life).

### Cited Findings
- Todd Howard on the line's origin: "This was late in the project. We wanted to have the guards to have more personality." "The guards say the most about you and what you've done." "It's certainly not intentional that it would catch on that way." "I don't think you hear it that much. It sticks out. It's got more flavor, so I think it sticks out." — [Kotaku, "How They Came Up With Skyrim's 'Arrow In The Knee' Line"](https://kotaku.com/how-they-came-up-with-skyrims-arrow-in-the-knee-line-5886648)
- The line was intended to be heard only once in a while, but players reported hearing it very often and it became 2011's defining game catchphrase. — [Wikipedia, "Arrow in the knee"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_in_the_knee) (search summary, not verified on page); [Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/i-took-an-arrow-in-the-knee). These sources conflict: Howard says "I don't think you hear it that much," while player accounts describe hearing it constantly. The likely resolution is Howard's own point that frequency is perceived, and distinctive lines are noticed and remembered far more than bland ones.
- Valve's fixes: context written back as memory, "said once" facts, expiration timers to keep running-gag beats apart, and general rules overridden by specific ones. — [Ruskin GDC 2012](https://archive.org/stream/valve-publications/2012/GDC2012_Ruskin_Elan_DynamicDialog_djvu.txt)
- Supergiant's fixes: enough unique and repeatable content that repeats come only after "20 or 30" loops, and repeatable lines written so they wouldn't feel repetitive on a second hearing. — [GeekDad, Kasavin](https://geekdad.com/2019/10/narrative-and-early-access-supergiants-greg-kasavin-discusses-hades-development/)
- High on Life: reviewers called the constant gun and enemy banter exhausting. Squanch Games offers a menu option to make guns and enemies talk less (story dialogue unchanged, random chatter reducible or disabled). — [GameSpot review](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/high-on-life-review-talking-heads/1900-6418014/); [Neowin review](https://www.neowin.net/reviews/high-on-life-review-disgustingly-funny-with-surprisingly-deep-combat/) (search summary, not verified on page)
- Context-blind barks: a gun "complaining about not being used in ages when I'd used them seconds ago." — [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/review/high-on-life/low-on-laughs)
- Replay makes repetition worse for comedy: Quinn noted jokes "might quickly fall flat when you play through them multiple times." — [Game Developer on Quinn's GDC talk](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-designing-funny-games-is-no-joke-but-it-can-be-done). The Stanley Parable builds replay into the joke by having the Narrator react to repeated loops. — [VideoChums review](https://videochums.com/review/the-stanley-parable-ultra-deluxe) (search summary, not verified on page)
- Co-op Portal 2 repeats information deliberately, and only the story points, because players talk over lines. — [Portal Wiki, Portal 2 developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/wiki/Portal_2_developer_commentary)

### Inferences
- Howard's "more flavor, so it sticks out" gives each line a repeat budget that depends on how distinctive it is. Specific, vivid remarks, memos and bill items get exactly one use per run, which the game already does. Plain filler can safely come back. When a pool runs out mid-run, falling back to deliberately plain lines is safer than recycling a vivid one.
- Separate what the player needs from what is there for fun. Valve repeats only information the player needs; jokes are never repeated. In this game, a rule's wording on a citation can repeat, because it is information. The memo's joke should not.
- Same seed means same week, so a player who replays a seed hears the same jokes, and Quinn's "fall flat when you play through them multiple times" applies. This is a design question to flag, not solve: options include accepting it, rotating line choice by a player-visible run counter, or having a character acknowledge the repeat as Stanley's Narrator does. The latter two would touch the determinism invariant and gameplay, so they need the owner's approval.
- A chatter setting like High on Life's is a patch for a pacing mistake. In this game, the PA already reads each announcement once, and the rejected scrolling ticker shows the same lesson. The research supports keeping every recurring UI voice to one reading, not adding a slider.

### Gaps
- Renee Gittins' "Arrow to the Knee: Dynamic Dialog Variation" (Spirit AI, Medium) returned 403. It likely covers variation techniques directly and is worth reading manually.
- No Bethesda statement was found on changing guard bark frequency after Skyrim. The claim that it was fixed later could not be sourced, so it is not made here.
- The High on Life chatter-setting details come from search summaries, not a page read.
