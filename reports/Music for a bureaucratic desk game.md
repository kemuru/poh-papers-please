# Give the clerk a clock, not a tune

The best fit is a dry chamber pulse in strict time, which this report calls **Registry Clockwork**. It is a plucked-string ostinato, a harmonium drone, a light pizzicato bass and soft brushes at **100–112 BPM in F major**, played completely straight. Melody is rationed to short fragments of a Ministry anthem that is heard whole only when the week ends. Both earlier scores failed where the research says they would. Slow, beatless, sparse music lowers arousal and matches the acoustic profile of sleep playlists. A short loop with a catchy tune, a cadence at the seam and a transient on every beat gives the ear the "landmarks" that composers blame for repetition fatigue. The case for a moderate pulse is reasonably strong. Tempo drives arousal but not mood. The music people choose for themselves during vigilance tasks averages **113–118 BPM** and reduces mind-wandering. Instrumental music at low volume costs reading comprehension almost nothing; **fast-and-loud** was the one condition that clearly hurt it. Comparable games and deadpan film scores follow the same craft: keep melody away from the reading task, let the institution's own sounds (clock chimes, stamps, typewriters) carry the comedy, and never react to the joke. In the last minute, time pressure should come as a one-shot Ministry chime, a nervous off-beat tick and a single **+6% tempo step**, not a continuous accelerando, because faster music buys speed at the cost of errors. All of it is cheap to synthesise in Web Audio from oscillators, noise and precomputed Karplus-Strong buffers. A seeded scheduler that works bar by bar keeps "same seed, same week" true. The specific numbers below are design inferences built on this evidence. None has been tested on this game, so the first playtest should compare Registry Clockwork directly with the current ambient score.

## Both failed scores sat where the research predicts failure

**Tempo is the main lever on arousal, and slow music turns it down.** In the cleanest test, Husain, Thompson and Schellenberg re-rendered the same Mozart sonata at 165 BPM and 60 BPM, in major and minor. Tempo had a large effect on arousal (F(1,32) = 91.82): arousal rose after the fast versions and *fell* after the slow ones. Mode changed mood but not arousal, and spatial scores were best after the fast major version ([Husain et al. 2002](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/Husain.pdf)). The same group had already argued that the "Mozart effect" is an artifact of arousal and mood ([Thompson et al. 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11437309/)). The physiology points the same way. Faster tempi raised heart rate, blood pressure and breathing with no habituation, and a **two-minute pause pushed all three below baseline** ([Bernardi et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600)). The current score is built from the opposite ingredients. Eno made *Music for Airports* to "induce calm and a space to think", wanted "a silence at least twice as long as the sound", and meant it to be "as ignorable as it is interesting" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_1:_Music_for_Airports)). An analysis of 225,626 tracks from Spotify sleep playlists found sleep music markedly softer (d = −1.25), lower in energy (d = −1.46), more instrumental and slower than general music, with "ambient" as the largest cluster ([Scarratt et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0278813)). The game's seven bell and choir voices, with no beat, one event every few seconds and nothing above 700 Hz (`src/ui/music.ts`), sit squarely in that profile. Inference: the players who called it sleepy were hearing the genre do what it was designed to do.

**A stamping desk is exactly where arousal-raising music helps.** In one study, people did a Psychomotor Vigilance Task in five blocks of about six minutes each, the length of one shift. Self-chosen music averaging **112.6 BPM** raised task-focus (0.62 vs 0.54) and cut mind-wandering (0.18 vs 0.27), though it did not change reaction time ([Kiss & Linnell 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8357712/)). Two online experiments with playlists averaging **116.5 and 117.6 BPM** found responses about 8 ms faster, less mind-wandering and more task-focus. Improved mood and arousal accounted for these effects. The authors frame the result with the inverted-U, in which "only an intermediate arousal level is linked to maximum performance" ([Scientific Reports 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11045806/)). On a simple letter-finding task, more complex music produced the best scores (97.52, against 91.31 for simple music and 91.00 for silence). The same music *hurt* performance on a complex word-pair task ([Gonzalez & Aiello 2019](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/2019-gonzalez.pdf)). That split matters here because the clerk's job is both at once: a repetitive stamping routine built around careful reading.

**For reading, the cost comes from loudness, event density and words, not from a pulse.** Thompson, Schellenberg and Letnic crossed tempo (110 vs 150 BPM) with level (60 vs 72.4 dB) while people read. Fast-and-soft gave the best comprehension (2.96 of 6, slightly above silence at 2.77). **Fast-and-loud was the only condition significantly worse than silence (1.95)**, and loudness made no difference at the slower tempo ([Thompson et al. 2012](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)). Their explanation is "a greater number of auditory events per unit time that are difficult to ignore". A Bayesian meta-analysis of 65 studies put music's overall cost to reading comprehension at a small g = −0.19, driven by lyrics. Non-lyrical music came out "very close to 0", no worse than noise ([Vasilev et al. 2018](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/29995/9/1745691617747398.pdf)). Individual differences exist, but the evidence for them is weak. Introverts have been found to suffer more from background music, but replications are inconsistent ([Küssner 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01991)). Inference: a clear pulse is fine and probably helpful, as long as the music stays quiet, has few note onsets and has no words. The existing Music switch should stay.

**The march failed on fatigue, and fatigue comes from landmarks.** Composer Winifred Phillips calls repetition fatigue "a HUGE issue" and says game music "must be composed so that it can be repeated indefinitely" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/composer-winifred-phillips-answers-reddit-s-questions-in-viral-ask-me-anything-about-video-game-music)). Her remedies are "slow textures, perpetual development, and variations" ([MAKE](https://www.makemag.com/double-review-composers-guide-to-game-music-and-sound-play/)), plus an "answer/question" structure whose loop seam does not fall on a cadence ([Cook, MTO 2023](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.23.29.3/mto.23.29.3.cook.html)). A secondhand source quotes her as recommending at least five minutes of music before it loops ([Audient](https://audient.com/tutorial/composing-music-video-games/)). One practitioner's scale rates three to five repeats as "increasingly problematic" and names melody as the most memory-intensive element ([Martins, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/rethinking-the-audio-loop-in-games)). A sound designer notes that any step that "stands out" becomes a pattern the player starts to track ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/game-audio-immersion/)). Repetition itself is normal: across 21,391 game pieces, 62.79% of clips repeat earlier material ([Hunt 2020](https://boblsturm.github.io/aimusic2020/papers/CSMC__MuMe_2020_paper_10.pdf)). The problem is repetition the player notices, and players respond to it by switching the music off ([Wharton & Collins 2011](https://gamestudies.org/1102/articles/wharton_collins)). Inference: the march broke every rule at once. A 27-second loop repeats about **13 times in a 6-minute shift**, far past the three-to-five threshold. Its tune was a landmark on every pass, its cadence marked the seam, and the click on every beat was a constant attention-grabbing transient of the kind Thompson blames for distraction.

The literature also has clear limits. No study compares a beatless ambient bed with a light pulse on a reading task. Nearly all tempo studies use classical or pop excerpts of 4–10 minutes, not hour-long synthetic textures. Nobody has measured how many minutes or loops it takes for a game loop to grate. A widely repeated "90–120 seconds" figure traces back to an AI-tool blog and should be ignored.

## Work games keep melody out of the booth and spend it at the doors

**Papers, Please has no music at the desk.** Lucas Pope said the choice started as a budget constraint: "I knew that I couldn't fill the entire game with music. So instead I focused on getting the right mix of ambient and triggered sounds in the booth" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-lucas-pope-s-i-papers-please-i-)). The booth's most common sounds are shuffling paper and clacking stamps. One critic reads the sparseness as foregrounding "the hollowness and mechanical nature of the task" ([The Gemsbok](https://thegemsbok.com/art-reviews-and-articles/video-game-reviews-mid-week-mission-papers-please-lucas-pope/)). Testers asked for sound effects, not music: "Looking forward to the CHUNK of the stamp" ([Pope devlog](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-03/)). The famous anthem plays on the title screen; sources disagree on whether it also plays over the end-of-day expenses. Pope built it to speed up as it goes: "the theme music changes tempo as it progresses. This gives the track a nice racing effect" ([Pope devlog](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)). Inference: the anthem gets away with being a short, hummable loop because nobody hears it for six minutes straight. That is exactly what went wrong when this game put a march under the shift.

**Pope's newest work sim does run a groove under the work, and it is not built on melody.** *Return of the Obra Dinn* keeps exploration silent and uses music as "dramatic punctuation" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-lucas-pope-s-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-)). *Mars After Midnight*, a game about answering a door, runs on "warbly synths and simple drum machine grooves" while its sound effects "guide your attention to the most important slice of gameplay". It scored 95/100 for sound ([RPGFan](https://www.rpgfan.com/review/mars-after-midnight/)). *Cook, Serve, Delicious! 3?!* has 102 minutes of music from a composer who "rarely sits with one idea for long". It is praised for "a constant light rhythmic motion that gently presses each cue forward, complementing the game's busy play without dulling its capacity to raise the pulse" ([The Greatest Game Music](https://www.greatestgamemusic.com/soundtracks/cook-serve-delicious-3-soundtrack/)). These are the closest precedents for what the brief asks for: music that keeps you alert without being tuneful.

**Document games often make the music partial or diegetic, and players punish music that never lets up.** In *Not Tonight*, the bouncer hears club music muffled until the door opens, when "the music volume increases ever-so-slightly" ([Hey Poor Player](https://www.heypoorplayer.com/2020/02/01/not-tonight-take-back-control-edition-review-switch/)). *Death and Taxes* ships "(Radio Effect)" mixes, a track called "Beats to Relax/Murder To", and three 42-second ending cues graded by outcome ([Bandcamp](https://adambow.bandcamp.com/album/death-and-taxes-original-soundtrack)). The criticised games share one failure. *Beholder*'s main theme "goes on and on and on" and "will drive you insane" ([Seafoam Gaming](https://seafoamgaming.com/2024/01/11/beholder-complete-edition-ps4-review/)). One player traced "a very distinct annoying grating sound" to the music track and switched the music off ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/475550/discussions/0/224446340335929507/)). Even *Death and Taxes*' jazz "can get a bit repetitive after a while" ([KeenGamer](https://www.keengamer.com/articles/reviews/pc-reviews/death-and-taxes-review-a-grim-job-but-a-great-game/)). Orwell switches between "utopian, contemplative, tense, and panicked tracks depending on the situation" ([Gamereactor](https://www.gamereactor.eu/orwell-review/)). Townscaper's designer left music out because the game "does not have any state changes it would make sense to fit music around" ([Stålberg](https://x.com/OskSta/status/1292481083972497408)). Inference: Proof of Humanity has plenty of states to score (morning, the open window, the last minute, court, accounts, endings), so its music should change with them.

**In games, music steps up at a deadline rather than ramping up.** Super Mario Bros. plays its overworld theme at about 100 BPM and speeds it up when fewer than 100 time units remain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Bros._theme)), after a short "Hurry Up" fanfare ([Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Hurry_Up), search summary). Overcooked reportedly steps its tempo up with 30 and 10 seconds left ([TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/SongsInTheKeyOfPanic), search summary, unverified). The experimental evidence shows why a game that rewards accuracy should keep the step small. In a driving simulator, faster background music raised driving speed and increased red-light violations and collisions ([Brodsky 2001](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1369847801000250)). Raising the tempo of high-arousal music produced "faster performance and increased inaccuracy" ([Cassidy & MacDonald 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9450.2010.00830.x)). After ten minutes of play, a shooting game's own soundtrack raised salivary cortisol compared with playing in silence ([Hébert et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lfs.2004.11.011)). In a Tetris variant whose music speeds up with the game, the five tempo presets made no difference to the final level reached (p = .809), and players who liked the music did better (p = .021) ([Hufschmitt et al. 2020](https://ieee-cog.org/2020/papers/paper_217.pdf)). The same group reports that a "synchronous and increasing tempo can increase player stress and degrade performance, while tempo that is out of sync with game actions or slowed down regularly can defuse stress" ([ACM IUI 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3397481.3450684)). These effects are marginal and the samples small.

Inference: a clerk who must read carefully under a clock should get a clear, one-off warning and a small lift, not a continuous speed-up tied to the countdown.

## Deadpan comedy keeps strict time and lets the institution play

**Wes Anderson's scores are deadpan because they are precise, and whimsical because of their instruments.** A critic describes *The Grand Budapest Hotel* as "metronomically precise rhythms and almost child-like music box instrumental ideas", built from zithers, cimbaloms, balalaikas, bells and whistlers ([Movie Music UK](https://moviemusicuk.us/2014/03/26/the-grand-budapest-hotel-alexandre-desplat/)). Desplat keeps the band thin: "I like to use just one of each instrument. I don't like to lose the melody in a large ensemble" ([Variety](https://variety.com/2021/artisans/news/french-dispatch-score-alexandre-desplat-randall-poster-1235094565/), search summary). He also keeps it sparse "to leave room for what's on screen". He built *The French Dispatch*'s "Police Cooking" on three repeated bass notes, because a "crazy full-speed bombastic score" would have made the scene impossible to follow ([Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/features/banjos-and-bassoons-how-the-french-dispatch-composer-alexandre-desplat-collaborates-with-wes-anderson/5166477.article); [The Credits](https://www.motionpictures.org/2021/11/how-composer-alexandre-desplat-put-a-dada-istic-spin-on-the-french-dispatch/)). He insists that "comic scores always have to have some melancholy in the background" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonrise_Kingdom_(score))). Mark Mothersbaugh reports that Anderson "didn't like bass sounds. He didn't like brass. He liked plucky instruments and bells and flutes and piccolos". He also says *The Life Aquatic* used an old Oberheim synthesizer with an **eight-step sequencer** ([Little White Lies](https://lwlies.com/articles/mark-mothersbaugh-on-working-with-wes-anderson/)). Moonrise Kingdom's Britten-style cue brings instruments in one by one ([HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/moonrise-kingdom-soundtrack-wes-anderson-desplat_n_1533430), search summary). Inference: the reusable recipe is a busy plucked ostinato, one clear high instrument for the melody, little bass or brass, one player per part, and a hint of minor inside a major key.

**The funniest comedy scores refuse to be comic.** Mickey-Mousing, music synced to action on screen, has been called "the most vulgar technique used in film music" (Cocteau), and it is "somewhat out of favor today... because of overuse" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mickey_Mousing)). Elmer Bernstein scored *Airplane!* as a disaster epic that "clashes hilariously with the visuals". It set the template for comedies that "play it straight while the world unravels" ([Score Catcher](https://scorecatcher.co.uk/blogs/news/cleared-for-takeoff-the-soaring-madness-of-airplane-and-its-deadpan-score)). For *The Death of Stalin*, Christopher Willis and Armando Iannucci talked about "a certain nervousness that there should be ticking away in the background". Willis chose a "mad and full-on" score, noting that "a more sober film would score those scenes with nothing at all" ([Substream](https://substreammagazine.com/2018/06/interview-composer-christopher-willis-death-of-stalin/)). One reviewer says the score "ignores that *The Death of Stalin* is a comedy" ([The Film Scorer](https://thefilmscorer.com/the-death-of-stalin-christopher-willis-2018/)). Inference: a Ministry that never jokes needs music that never jokes. The raccoons in the trench coat are the joke, and a bassoon honk when they arrive would be the Ministry winking. It would also break a design invariant. Only the evidence may reveal whether an applicant is valid, and music that reacts to an applicant could give that away.

**Bureaucracy satire borrows the institution's own machinery.** Ronnie Hazlehurst built the *Yes Minister* theme from the Westminster chimes: "That's all it is. It's the easiest thing I've ever done." Each Westminster quarter is a permutation of four bells in E major, three quarter notes and a half note ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Quarters)). Terry Gilliam kept Sousa's "The Liberty Bell" for Monty Python partly because its opening bell strike felt like "getting straight down to business" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Liberty_Bell_(march))). Michael Kamen's *Brazil* score uses typewriters as the rhythm of "Central Services (The Office)" and has a "humorously accelerating" elevator cue ([Filmtracks](https://www.filmtracks.com/titles/brazil.html)). Leonard Maltin called Jacques Tati the "only man in movie history to get a laugh out of the hum of a neon sign!" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playtime)). *Severance* goes further and makes its office uncanny with a single instrument. Its theme is a four-chord piano loop, and its "radio static" is a reversed piano note chopped into stutters. Theodore Shapiro wanted "that piano glitching and falling apart" as the season goes on ([SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/826702/severance-composer-on-how-that-eerie-theme-came-together-and-the-easter-egg-you-probably-didnt-catch-interview/)).

**Muzak already invented the Ministry's music, including a rule against fatigue.** Muzak's trademarked Stimulus Progression programmed music in 15-minute blocks that got "faster in tempo and louder and brassier" to speed workers up. Each block was followed by 15 minutes of silence, which the company said "limited listener fatigue" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzak)). It avoided vocals because "lyrics might knock a worker or a patron out of the rhythm" ([Red Bull Music Academy](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2012/09/history-of-muzak/)). Early hold-music cartridges would "stretch, warp and disintegrate, producing ominous, warbling audio", and hold music is "the soundtrack of modern bureaucracy" ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/a-brief-history-of-hold-music-from-early-patents-to-the-soundtrack-of-customer-service-purgatory-180989185/)). Inference: the game's existing idea that "the tape wears out with the week" has a real institutional precedent. Stimulus Progression also gives an in-character reason for the music to creep faster day by day. The Muzak sound itself (soft strings, bossa nova, DX7 bells) is a weaker choice for the bed. It is bland by design, and the joke wears out within a minute.

## Web Audio can voice a chamber band from oscillators and noise

**The one hard constraint is plucked strings, and it has a simple workaround.** The obvious way to build a Karplus-Strong string is a noise burst circulating through a `DelayNode` feedback loop. That runs into a spec rule: "If DelayNode is part of a cycle, then the value of the delayTime attribute is clamped to a minimum of one render quantum" ([Web Audio API spec](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/)). A render quantum defaults to 128 frames. The arithmetic puts the highest reachable pitch at about **345 Hz at 44.1 kHz** (375 Hz at 48 kHz). A CodePen experiment reported the top pitch as "around E4" ([CodePen](https://codepen.io/tonywallace/pen/dvxowM), search summary). Tone.js moved its comb filter to an AudioWorklet for exactly this reason ([Tone.js](https://github.com/Tonejs/Tone.js/blob/dev/Tone/component/filter/FeedbackCombFilter.ts)). Without a worklet, the practical route is to run the algorithm in plain JavaScript into an `AudioBuffer`, once per pitch, and play it with an `AudioBufferSourceNode`. The basic averaging loop "sounds like a string being plucked at frequency f_s/(N+1/2)" ([Ben Lynn](http://crypto.stanford.edu/~blynn/sound/karplusstrong.html)). JOS's pick-position comb and dynamic-level lowpass set the twang and the brightness ([JOS, CCRMA](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Extended_Karplus_Strong_Algorithm.html)). By inference, a loss factor applied once per period sets the decay, and the source node's `detune` corrects the rounding of the period to whole samples. Seeded from the project's own RNG, two or three buffers per pitch give pizzicato, harpsichord, cimbalom-like multi-string notes and balalaika tremolo.

**Everything else is textbook.** Mallets are additive: measured marimba partials run **1 : 3.92 : 9.24** and xylophone partials 1 : 3.00 : 6.16 ([Euphonics](https://euphonics.org/3-3-marimbas-and-xylophones/)), and the vibraphone is tuned 1:4:10 with a motor tremolo of 1–12 Hz ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibraphone)). FM with simple ratios cannot reproduce these partials, so FM is best kept for celesta and bell colours, like the game's existing 1:1 FM bell. Woodwinds follow Sound On Sound's rules: closed pipes give odd harmonics, so square waves read as clarinet and narrow pulses as oboe or bassoon, and brightness should rise and fall with loudness ([SOS](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/synthesizing-wind-instruments)). An FM clarinet in the DSP First style works with a carrier-to-modulator ratio of 3:2, which by arithmetic gives only odd harmonics ([DSP First](https://dspfirst.gatech.edu/chapters/03spect/labsLV/lab05/lab05.pdf)). A harmonium or accordion is two or three detuned sawtooth reeds; published musette detunings are 2 cents (concert), 4 (swing), 15 (German/Italian) and 18 (French) ([Fort Bend Accordion Club](https://fortbendaccordionclu.wixsite.com/accordion/tuning), search summary). Percussion has exact published starting points. The 808 hi-hat is six square waves at 40 Hz × [2, 3, 4.16, 5.43, 6.79, 8.21] through a 10 kHz bandpass and a 7 kHz highpass ([Joe Sullivan](http://joesul.li/van/synthesizing-hi-hats/)), a snare is drum-head modes near 180 and 330 Hz plus band-limited noise ([SOS](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/practical-snare-drum-synthesis)), and claves are a triangle wave with a very short decay ([SOS](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/synthesizing-cowbells-claves)). The desk's own sounds (woodblocks, clock ticks, typewriter keys, the rubber-stamp thud) are an impulse into a narrow bandpass filter or a falling sine, tuned by ear; no acoustic measurements of them were found.

**The mixing rules are few and firm.** Sources are fire-and-forget: the context releases a finished `AudioBufferSourceNode` and the nodes it feeds ([Web Audio API spec](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/)). Keep the one existing hall, because a `ConvolverNode` is "very expensive" while biquad filters are cheap at five multiplications and four additions per sample ([Adenot](https://padenot.github.io/web-audio-perf/)). Soften the compressor: the `DynamicsCompressorNode` defaults (threshold −24 dB, ratio 12, knee 30) ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/DynamicsCompressorNode/DynamicsCompressorNode)) will flatten quiet material and make it pump, and inference suggests −12 dB, 3:1 and a knee of 6–10 dB instead. Release every note with `setTargetAtTime`, because an exponential ramp cannot reach zero ([spec](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/); [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioParam/setTargetAtTime)).

**Scheduling mostly extends what the game already does.** Chris Wilson's "two clocks" pattern uses a coarse timer to book notes on the audio clock, with each step picking up "the CURRENT tempo value" ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/audio-scheduling)). The game already schedules this way, booking 1.5 s ahead every 250 ms, so a tempo step should take effect at the next bar line and never rewrite notes already booked. The same lookahead means an unplanned scene change, such as the queue running out early, would be heard late; by inference, fade the old scene's bus over about 150 ms and start the new scene's bus 0.1 s later. Keep rhythm-part jitter under **about 6 ms**, roughly the smallest timing deviation listeners can detect ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01709/full), search summary). `OfflineAudioContext` renders "(potentially) faster than real-time" ([spec](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/)), and the game's voice functions already accept `BaseAudioContext`, so a test can render a seeded day and check its levels. Seeding a fresh RNG for each bar and layer from (day, scene, bar) makes live and offline output identical however the timer jitters (inference).

## Registry Clockwork: a 108 BPM band that wears out by Humanity Day

**The recommended direction: a small, dry chamber band keeps strict, quiet time under the desk. The Ministry's clock chime, not a tune, tells the player how much time is left, and the band slowly wears out as Humanity Day approaches.** It keeps the one good idea from the Eno score, loops of different lengths that never line up again. It adds the pulse the research says a monotonous desk needs. It takes its tone from Anderson's instruments and Hazlehurst's chime, and borrows Muzak's Stimulus Progression for the week.

The alternatives each fail on one point. **Silence with desk sounds** (the Papers, Please approach) is safest for reading comprehension. But Pope's silence began as a budget decision, and silence relaxes the listener, the opposite of what the brief asks. **A Severance-style piano loop** is the best "corporate uncanny". Its minor-key dread tilts toward horror rather than affection, so it suits the court, not the desk. **A Ministry Muzak PA** is the funniest pitch, but hold music is bland on purpose and the joke wears off fast. Its logic survives here; its sound does not.

### Nothing the ear can use as a landmark

The variation scheme combines three proven techniques. The first is Eno's trick, layer cycles of different lengths: the pluck rhythm pattern repeats every 7 bars, the marimba counter-line every 9, the brushes every 11 and the bass fills every 13, so the full texture only comes round again after 9,009 bars, about **5.6 hours at 108 BPM**. The second is vertical layering, which Phillips used so that players sometimes hear "only the rhythm elements… sometimes only the synths… sometimes the whole mix" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/pure-vertical-layering-for-game-music-composers-from-spyder-to-sackboy-gdc-2021-)); here a seeded choice brings one colour layer in or out every 16 bars, while the pulse and the harmonium stay on. The third treats melody as an event. A pool of eight "memos", fragments of 2–4 bars cut from the Ministry anthem, plays one every 40–80 seconds, five to nine per shift. Each is transposed to the current chord, played on one solo instrument, and ends on the 2nd, 5th or 6th degree rather than the tonic. No fragment plays twice in a shift in the same key on the same instrument. The melody is therefore silent more than four-fifths of the time, in the spirit of the *Skyward Sword* practice of alternating versions with and without the melody ([Martins](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/rethinking-the-audio-loop-in-games)).

About every two minutes the band thins to pulse and harmonium for 4–8 bars. This rest keeps the pulse, because full silence relaxes the listener (Bernardi). Each bar and layer is seeded from (day, scene, bar, layer) through `src/gen/rng.ts`. That keeps the music reproducible and follows the architecture rule that all randomness goes through that module.

// Registry Clockwork: starting values (all inference; tune by ear, then playtest)

The chime is an original permutation of four F-major bell pitches in the Westminster rhythm (three quarter notes and a half note). The 18th-century Westminster Quarters are very likely public domain, but that was not verified, and an original permutation avoids the question. The morning strike count is a flourish that also carries information: the Gazette already counts down, and on day 7 the clock strikes nothing. Following the project's writing rule, cut it if it does not land in playtesting.

### The last sixty seconds

Days 2–6 have a 6-minute clock, so the ending can be booked in advance on bar boundaries. At the **halfway mark (T−180 s)** the chime plays one permutation quietly under the band: a time signal, not a landmark. At **T−60 s** the score follows the Mario pattern. The bed drops out for two bars while the chime plays a different, louder permutation, and the band returns **6% faster** (108 → 114 BPM on day 4) with the memos, marimba and brushes gone. In their place a woodblock tick-tock plays on the off-beat eighths at about −24 dB, Willis's "nervousness ticking away in the background". The tick deliberately avoids the clock's once-a-second rhythm, following the Sonotris finding that tempo out of sync with the game defuses stress. At **T−15 s** the pluck fills all eight eighths and its lowpass opens from about 1.2 kHz to 3 kHz, adding at most 2–3 dB. At **T−0**, or when the last applicant leaves, one low bell strike closes the window and hands over to the closing scene.

The evidence supports the shape: a one-off warning plus a modest lift, faster music costing accuracy, and fast-and-loud music hurting reading. The numbers are inference. The escalation replaces layers rather than adding them, so density peaks near 7 onsets per second only in the final 15 seconds. The game's own rule that timed-out applicants go home without penalty keeps the music's pressure free of punishment.

### The week wears the band

| Day | New colour (replaces onsets, never adds density) | Tempo | Tape (existing `WOW` / `DRIFT`, cents) | Other wear |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| 2 | + brushes | 104 | 0 / 0 | First last-minute sequence |

| 4 | + bassoon memos | 108 | 7 / 4 | The voices drift apart |
| 5 | + clarinet memos | 110 | 10 / 6 | A note snags and plays twice, on the day of the duplicates rule. The harmonium loses a reed after Window 2 forks away with half the stationery |
| 6 | + whistled memos | 112 | 13 / 9 | Notes go missing (the existing 1 in 7); timing σ loosens to about 10 ms |
| 7 (no clock) | Full band | 108 | 17 / 12 | Humanity Day: the whole anthem finally plays at five o'clock |

Two ideas drive this table. First, the band builds up the way Moonrise Kingdom's cue does, gaining one colour per new rule. But day 6 has the hardest reading of the week, and complex music hurt complex tasks (Gonzalez & Aiello), so new instruments join the rotation instead of stacking on top. Second, the anthem becomes the week's payoff. Players hear its fragments as memos all week and hear it whole only on the last day, which turns the fatigue rule (ration the melody) into part of the story. The wear must stay "funny, never harsh", as the design notes put it. Beholder's "grating sound" is the cautionary tale, so the wear never adds noise bursts, and pitch never drifts beyond the existing 17 cents.

### How to know it works

Keep the schedule a pure function of (scene, day, bar), as `notesBetween` is today, so unit tests can check the rules directly: no memo repeats within a shift, no bar has more than six onsets per second, no tonic chord falls on an 8-bar seam, percussion never sounds on every beat for more than four bars, and the same seed gives the same notes. An offline render of a seeded day can also check peak and RMS levels and confirm silence after the stop.

None of that shows the music is right. The real test is a playtest that compares it with the current ambient score, measuring accuracy per shift, seconds per applicant, self-reported sleepiness and how often players switch the music off. Include a "focus" variant (pulse and harmonium only) for players who find any music distracting. This recommendation also changes two statements in `notes/game-design.md`: "nothing above about 700 Hz" and the Music for Airports model. It needs the owner's sign-off before implementation.

## Conclusion

The real choice was never between ambient and march. The research separates two things the earlier scores bundled together. Arousal comes mostly from the pulse; fatigue comes from landmarks such as tunes, cadences and hard transients. The ambient score removed the landmarks and the pulse with them. The march supplied the pulse and a landmark every 27 seconds. The useful design move is to budget the ear's attention: spend a steady, quiet pulse to keep the clerk alert, and use landmarks only where they tell the player something. The chime means time is running out, the bell means the window has shut, and the whole anthem means the week is over. That lines up with the deadpan rule from film. The music answers to the clock and the institution, never to the applicant, so the Ministry keeps a straight face and the jokes stay in the evidence.

The weak point is the numbers. Every tempo, decibel and density figure here is extrapolated from lab studies of classical piano and pop excerpts, which are nothing like a synthesised chamber band heard for an hour. Nobody has measured when a game loop starts to grate. The design makes those figures cheap to change, because the tempo, onset budget, memo rate and last-minute step are each a single constant. The first playtest with real players should tune them.
