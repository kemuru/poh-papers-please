# Let the first robot in, then legislate

The cleanest week gives each day exactly one new rule, numbered for its day, and lets the first home robot through lawfully. Day 1 should teach only the phrase and Inspect. The day-1 Likeness unit already says the phrase word for word, so if no rule reads its light yet, it is valid, and the desk's existing reply, "no rule in force covers it. Yet.", does the teaching. The next morning the Ministry answers with **Rule 2, the face**, which folds today's photo rule and Rule 0's light and same-face checks into one rule whose stated cause is that registration, shown in the robot's own frame; Rule 0's day-6-only checks move into Rule 6, the day their only offenders first appear. Keep the robots and keep the light. Of every candidate in the evidence it alone is familiar (a TV remote's flash seen through a phone camera), visible in three stills, switched by the eyelids so nothing worn can fake it, and free of any body feature, which is the kind of binary, taught, innocent-proof tell players trust; but word it as a fact about humans ("a human face gives off no light") and keep the physics out of the rulebook. Replace the Gazette's 90 to 125 words of prose with a one-photograph front page of about 20 legible words that carries yesterday's consequence, and put each new rule in the rulebook as a pasted slip with its one-line cause, as Papers, Please moved its rules out of the bulletin. For the look, draw the whole desk on the portraits' 2× pixel grid, with self-made pixel fonts compiled at build time, an integer-snapped stage and one master palette whose only saturated violet is the lamp, which also stops the smallest tell from blurring. The rule changes need the owner's sign-off on the rulebook, the `RuleId` API and a batch of tests; their smallest version keeps the three costliest transcript seed pins intact, while anything that adds a random draw on day 1 puts every pin in the suite at risk.

## Day one should teach one rule and let the first robot through

Today's tutorial day carries far more than the benchmark's. Rule 0 carries day 0 in `RULE_DAYS`, so day 1's book has two pages, opens at Rule 0 at every call, and reaches Rule 1 only when the guided Inspect turns it ([judge.ts](../src/rules/judge.ts#L11); [Shift.tsx](../src/ui/Shift.tsx#L91)). The welcome letter spends a paragraph on the robots' infrared night lamp ([gazette.ts](../src/content/gazette.ts#L151)), and the third applicant is a unit that only the lamp catches ([day.ts](../src/gen/day.ts#L247)). A new player therefore meets **four new things on day 1: the stamps, the video stills, Rule 1 with a guided Inspect, and Rule 0 with its own physics**, at the edge of the roughly four chunks working memory holds ([Cowan 2001](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496/S0140525X01003922a.pdf/the-magical-number-4-in-short-term-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity.pdf)). Papers, Please asks one glance question on its first day, "Entry is restricted to Arstotzkan citizens only" ([Official Bulletin](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Official_Bulletin), fan wiki), and critics remember it as the day with "only one rule" ([Hardcore Gaming 101](https://www.hardcoregaming101.net/papers-please/)).

Papers, Please also shows what a standing rule does the first time it needs evidence. "Entrant must have a passport" sat in its rulebook from day 1 but was first enforced on day 3, when Jorji handed over nothing, and that moment became the game's worst onboarding cliff. Pope wrote that the shift to "Get nothing, inspect certain rulebook rule to proceed" was, for some players, "so far off the radar as to be nonexistent. It even looks like a bug", and it took several beta builds of hints before one worked ([Pope devlog, Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)); the shipped hint prints when the player closes the shutter or waits too long ([Day 3](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_3), fan wiki). Rule 0 is that kind of rule, always in force and read from its own evidence, and this game enforces it on the tutorial day. Its label adds noise of its own: in tabletop culture "rule zero" is the rule above the rules, such as "the GM is always right" ([RPG Museum](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Rule_zero)).

The learning evidence points the same way on order. In an experiment with **over 45,000 players**, tutorials had "a surprisingly negligible effect" in two genre-typical games, and instruction given at the moment of need raised play time 16% and progress 40% only in the one unconventional game ([Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)). The lamp is this game's least conventional idea, so it deserves teaching in context, not a paragraph read before the first applicant; "learners at every level of experience try to avoid reading" ([Carroll and Rosson](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/4.mental.mental.carroll.paradox.pdf)). Classroom studies favour meeting a case before hearing the principle: a meta-analysis of 53 studies found a moderate advantage for problem-first teaching, g = 0.36 ([Sinha and Kapur 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105)), students who compared cases before a lecture predicted outcomes "substantially better" a week later ([Schwartz and Bransford](https://aaalab.stanford.edu/papers/time_for_telling.pdf)), and curiosity improves memory for the answer weeks later ([Kang et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/)). None of these studies tested a game rule, and the effect depends on the learner noticing the case. Outer Wilds' creative lead found that an oddity only intrigues players who already know what is normal around it ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/live-die-repeat-how-i-outer-wilds-i-piques-curiosity-in-an-ambivalent-solar-system)).

Papers, Please's best-remembered rules arrive the morning after something that was legal under yesterday's rules. On day 5 Vince Lestrade, wanted for murder in that morning's paper, arrives with valid papers, and as the fan wiki puts it, "There is no rule against admitting criminals yet". The day-6 bomber's papers are valid too, and day 7's bulletin opens "Kolechian extremists are suspected in yesterday's bombing" ([Day 5](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_5); [Day 7](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/Day_7), fan wiki). Pope's stated aim was "to motivate each new mechanic with a story element" while avoiding "having too many things happen at once" ([Pope devlog, Nov 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-10/)), and this game's design already promises each rule "its reason, usually something that got registered the day before" ([game-design.md](../notes/game-design.md)). Today's day-1 unit can never play that part, because Rule 0 already makes it illegal.

The recommended week dissolves Rule 0 and lets the first unit in. On day 1 the book has one page, Rule 1, and opens there. The unit says the phrase word for word and blinks, as designed, and its brightest light shows in frame 3. A player who points at the lit frame and a dark one gets the line the desk already has, "These disagree, but no rule in force covers it. Yet." ([desk.ts](../src/content/desk.ts#L6)), the same flow already tested for the Influencer's filter on day 1 ([slice4.spec.ts](../e2e/slice4.spec.ts#L214)). The next morning Rule 2 arrives as one rule about one thing, the face: the photo is the face in the video, facing the camera and not mirrored, and in every frame, eyes open or shut, it is the same human face and gives off no light. Its cause is the registration the player just made. Rule 0's other checks go where their offenders are. The Deepfake, whose face changes in one frame, falls under "the same face in every frame", so the photo check no longer has to skip that frame on purpose ([photo.ts](../src/rules/photo.ts#L1)). The Cardboard Cutout is already caught by Rule 6, because a picture does not blink, so the "picture held up" line can go; the Agent's generator mark becomes a Rule 6 check, "filmed, not generated". All three appear only on day 6 and in the night shift ([day.ts](../src/gen/day.ts#L155)). **Every check now arrives on the day its first offender does, and Rule N arrives on day N.** The Cutout also stops breaking two rules, and if the Agent keeps a single fault as well, the design's only two exceptions to one fault per applicant disappear ([week.test.ts](../src/gen/week.test.ts#L21)).

This order honours the owner's 29 September finding that "a light with no reason reads as an arbitrary rule" ([game-design.md](../notes/game-design.md)) better than the letter does, because no rule is ever enforced before its reason and on day 1 the light decides nothing. The only surprise left is a story one, and it lands on the Ministry, not the player. The real policy has always demanded "a real human and not a computer-generated person or avatar" ([PoH v2 policy](https://cdn.kleros.link/ipfs/Qmbd9QuiJ6B74faz9qqfpatU3aB5VCtmEkTf1BSZ3vk588)), so the book can open with that sentence as the registry's purpose rather than as a numbered rule, and day 2's notice can report, deadpan, that the registry has always been for real humans and from today the Ministry checks.

Of the four ways the code allows Rule 0 to be restructured, the recommendation is option (a) plus the one gain of option (b). Option (d) changes only the book's presentation and touches no seeds, but day 1 still has two rules, and it undoes the 29 September fix that made day 1 open at Rule 0 ([acceptance.md](../notes/acceptance.md)). Option (b) moves the whole check to day 6, which leaves days 1 to 3 with one fake each, turns the three brightest lamps into non-events and counts a perfect clerk's three correct stamps toward the Replaced ending by day 3 unless the count changes ([week.ts](../src/ui/week.ts#L248)). Option (c) scraps Rule 0 and catches each unit by the day's newest rule, as Jorji is caught. It needs new unit faults on five days, although the design has units say the phrase word for word and Pat already owns day 1's phrase fault; it leaves the Deepfake with no rule, idles every older rule until day 6, and removes day 7's hardest catch ([humanityDay.test.ts](../src/gen/humanityDay.test.ts#L36)).

Letting the unit in has consequences the code already exposes, and each has a small fix. **Every week will register it**, by stamp or by a court whose jurors "never find a fault that is not there" ([jury.ts](../src/court/jury.ts#L1)), so every player gets the day-2 cause. The Replaced count takes every accepted unit, whatever it broke ([week.ts](../src/ui/week.ts#L268)), so it must count only units that broke a rule, or a perfect clerk starts the week one step from Replaced. The paper picks a unit story only when the unit broke something ([gen/gazette.ts](../src/gen/gazette.ts#L106)), so it needs a branch for the lawful robot, including one that credits a clerk who flagged it. A hunch against it costs the 15 PNK deposit on a day nobody can be fired ([economy.ts](../src/economy/economy.ts#L8)) and gets a generic dismissal, since the court has no unit line for that case ([verdicts.ts](../src/content/verdicts.ts#L96)); a line saying that no rule in force covers a light keeps the failure institutional rather than personal, which is where players draw the line between a fair loss and "injustice" ([Juul, via Liz England](https://lizengland.com/blog/2016/02/review-the-art-of-failure-by-jesper-juul/)). The first two applicants should blink with no light, so frame 3 has a normal to break. And the first lamp a rule reads should be the week's biggest: today day 2's unit gets 'glow', which jurors find only 'sometimes' ([portraits.ts](../src/content/portraits.ts#L47); [jury.ts](../src/court/jury.ts#L52)), so the day-2 unit should take the 'bloom'.

Two tuning questions remain. With the unit valid, day 1 is 4 of 5 valid against a test that demands exactly 3 of 5 ([day.test.ts](../src/gen/day.test.ts#L28)), and the player never catches a phrase fault without help. The better version adds a sixth, scripted applicant with an ordinary slip such as "in this ministry", a listed Rule 1 offender, built the way `unitOn` builds the unit, without the week's random numbers ([day.ts](../src/gen/day.ts#L453)). Day 1 then has 4 of 6 valid, inside the design's 65 to 75% band, with one guided and one unguided catch. That it leaves the random stream untouched is an inference, to be verified with a days-1-to-6 hash against `main`. Day 2 then carries one new rule with two checks plus the clock. Those are the same checks today's day-2 player already runs, one of them newly taught; Contraband Police's players punished a first day after training that "doesn't cover even half" ([Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198141274464/recommended/756800/)), so if a first session shows strain, delay the clock by a day rather than split the rule.

## The night light is the only tell that passes all four tests

The evidence does not support scrapping the robots or their tell. Across 16 video games and one tabletop game, players' trust splits on how a tell behaves, not on what it depicts. Tells that are **binary, taught before they are tested and never shown by an innocent** are treated as proof even when they are pure lore: That's Not My Neighbor's players rely on the call home because "doppelgangers cannot answer phones" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3431040/discussions/0/550107357479186440/)), and Phasmophobia's six-fingered print is "an instant confirmation" ([games.gg](https://games.gg/phasmophobia/guides/obake-in-phasmophobia-evidence-behavior/)). Tells that are probabilistic, discovered or shared with innocents produce the fairness threads. Four months after No, I'm Not a Human's launch, a guide still filed **34 characters as "Potential Visitor"** because "the signs and dialogue remain the same" ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/all-no-im-not-a-human-visitors-and-how-to-identify-them/)), and Quarantine Zone's players opened a thread called "Symptoms are poorly defined" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3419520/discussions/0/689746766007964649/)). Developers treat an innocent who shows a tell as a bug: That's Not My Neighbor patched a real neighbour whose raised eyebrows were "misurderstood as a doppelganger" ([itch.io](https://nachogames.itch.io/thats-not-my-neighbor/devlog/808312/patch-1202)). The unit's light is in the trusted group by construction. It is lit in exactly the frames where the eyes are shut and never in the photo, in colours no skin, hair or clothing uses; nobody wears anything point-like between the brows; and a test checks that the drawn light sits in exactly the frames the rule names ([fairClue.test.ts](../src/fairClue.test.ts#L38); [game-design.md](../notes/game-design.md)).

It is also better grounded in reality than the design assumed. Watching a TV remote's invisible flash through a phone camera is standard consumer advice, and infrared through a phone "will typically be purple but sometimes can look white", which is the lamp's violet-white; many phones filter infrared on the main camera, but "very few of them have filters on the front camera", the one a selfie-style video is shot with ([How-To Geek](https://www.howtogeek.com/411095/how-to-detect-hidden-surveillance-cameras-with-your-phone/)). Apple's own Face ID page warns that "some cameras might detect infrared light" ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102381)). Night-vision cameras switch their infrared on when the lens goes dark, but real ones wait first, **1 to 600 seconds, with defaults of 3 to 5**, so that passing headlights do not flip them ([Axis](https://developer.axis.com/vapix/network-video/daynight-api/)). A unit that lights on every blink therefore has no delay at all, which makes the older units' flaw a real defect, and "added a delay before night vision switches on" a real patch note for the current model on days 4 and 5. Near-infrared passes through eyelids better than visible light ([EurekAlert, on a 2025 Cell study](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1084046)). Real humanoid heads already carry near-infrared projectors that are "invisible to the human eye while still being visible to the IR sensor" ([RealSense](https://dev.realsenseai.com/docs/projectors/); [Unitree G1 listing](https://www.robotshop.com/products/unitree-intel-realsense-d435i-depth-camera-g1-humanoid-robot)), and Columbia's Emo robot has a camera inside each pupil ([Columbia Engineering](https://www.engineering.columbia.edu/about/news/robot-can-you-say-cheese)). Only the switch tied to the eyelids is invented.

| Candidate tell | The sentence a player needs | Shows in three phone stills | Switches between frames | Body-feature risk | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Night vision on at a blink, seen like a TV remote's flash | "When it blinks, its night vision comes on; a phone sees it." | Yes, on a front camera | Yes: lit only with the eyes shut | None: a device | **Keep** |
| Paperwork: a maker's vouch, a factory face on file | "Its maker vouched for it." | In the registry, not the stills | Not applicable | None | Keep for days 4 and 5 |
| Ripples from filming a screen | "Film a screen and you get ripples." | Yes, but reads as fabric at 40×48 | No | None | Only for a screen-replay applicant |
| Pulse in the skin | "A live face flushes with each heartbeat." | No: about half a grey level | Not applicable | High | Reject |
| Warmth | "A live face is warm." | No: needs a thermal camera | Not applicable | High | Reject |
| A legal duty to disclose | "By law, a machine must tell you." | No: it lives in the transcript | Not applicable | None | News, not evidence |

The pulse figure comes from MIT's video-magnification work, where blood flow changes a face's colour by "typically only half a gray-level" ([Communications of the ACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/eulerian-video-magnification-and-analysis/)). Paperwork catches are the most trusted tells in the survey, and the build already has two: the day-4 unit vouched for by its maker and the day-5 unit whose factory face is on file, which are That's Not My Neighbor's call home and Papers, Please's wanted poster in registry form ([day.ts](../src/gen/day.ts#L467)). They should stay, but not replace the light. The best-received games pair a certain paperwork channel with one signature tell (the folders plus the phone, the documents plus the scanner), and a line caught only by paperwork is just another invalid applicant with a backstory, which is what option (c) would make of the units.

What should change is how the light is worded and taught. A rule framed as a condition for a benefit is policed far better than an abstract one: **fewer than 10%** solve Wason's abstract selection task, while most solve the same logic framed as "If you are drinking alcohol, then you must be over 18" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wason_selection_task)). Features that fit what people already know are learned faster ([Murphy and Allopenna](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8064251/)), and a cue defined by something present is learned more easily than one defined by something missing ([Newman, Wolff and Hearst](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7437120/)). "A human face gives off no light" meets all three tests: it is a condition for an income, nobody needs telling that faces do not glow, and it points at a light that is there. "A light between the brows means a robot" is monster trivia that invites the question "why there?", the kind of clue Ronald Knox's fair-play rules banned because it needs "a long scientific explanation at the end" ([TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/FairPlayWhodunnit)). So Rule 2's text and figure should talk about faces ("Eyes shut. No light." against "Eyes shut. A light."), the word "robot" should live in the news, and the physics should shrink to one optional line in the morning paper: the TV-remote comparison, which a player can check at home in ten seconds. "Night vision" should read faster than "night lamp", since people know that cameras switch to night vision, but that is untested.

The light can keep shrinking through the week, but never in certainty. The Exit 8's designer admitted one misjudgement, an advert that grew gradually and was "harder to understand than intended" ([Famitsu](https://www.famitsu.com/article/202508/50859)), while Phasmophobia keeps one signature that survives even its zero-evidence setting ([Game Rezone](https://game-rezone.com/en/guide/phasmophobia-how-to-identify-the-mimic/)). The day-7 slit of four pixels is the case to test first, at real size, by a player given no hint, with day 6's 'small' light as the floor if it fails. Moving the light to a new place would make yesterday's lesson wrong, the feeling No, I'm Not a Human's players put as "Signs just don't matter at all?" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3180070/discussions/0/597412189643635876/)). Colour must never carry it alone: Contraband Police's colour-coded forgeries left a colour-blind player unable to check them without the automated checker ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/756800/discussions/0/3809533595055994507/)), so the lamp's bright core in a dark ring should stay a luminance step, checked by a colour-blind player and not only in simulation. Courtesy must never become a tell either. Among more than 1.5 million Human or Not players, polite ones were recognised as human only 69.2% of the time, against 73% for humans generally ([Jannai et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20010)).

The case for scrapping the robots is that they are the most expensive idea in the game: a rule with its own lore, crowding the tutorial. The restructure answers that without losing what they carry, which is the week's main thread, Likeness's offer, the Replaced and Headhunted letters, and a satire that 2025 and 2026 keep writing. In July 2025 an AI agent ticked "Verify you are human" while narrating "This step is necessary to prove I'm not a bot" ([Futurism](https://futurism.com/chatgpt-agent-captcha)), and since 2 August 2026 the EU AI Act has required AI systems that interact with people to say so, "unless this is obvious" ([AI Act, Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). Both belong in the paper as world-building. Neither belongs in the evidence, where a spoken disclosure would let a remark decide a verdict.

## A photograph carries the morning and the rulebook carries the rule

The Registry Gazette prints **90 to 125 legible words of prose** each morning: a headline of 7 to 10 words, a report of about 20, a rule notice of 21 to 37, a thread item of up to 54 and a small notice ([gazette.ts](../src/content/gazette.ts)). The sticky note, the rulebook and, on day 6, nearly a PA line then say the rule again ([hall.ts](../src/content/hall.ts)). Papers, Please's paper, which this one borrows, prints headlines of four or five words such as "Terrorists Strike At Grestin!", and "the actual text beyond headlines and subheadings is not legible to the player" ([The Truth of Arstotzka](https://papersplease.fandom.com/wiki/The_Truth_of_Arstotzka), fan wiki). Even that paper comes up in **1.2% of 661 substantive positive English Steam reviews, against 22.5% for the family and 22.2% for the endings** ([Steam review API](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/239030?json=1&language=english&num_per_page=0)). A mention rate is not a reading rate, but the paper is plainly not what players carry away. Pope never let a rule live in print alone. Bulletins were "basically ignored when you've got someone to talk to right away" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)), and late in development he gave each rule "a place in the rulebook" so that it "disappears from the bulletin on the next day" ([Pope devlog, Nov 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-10/)).

Reading research sets the budget. Web readers have time for **at most 28% of a page's words, and 20% is more likely**; on pages of 111 words or fewer they read about half ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)). Print readers enter a page through its largest image and read the headline before the text ([EyeTrack07, via OJR](https://www.ojr.org/early-lessons-from-poynters-eyetrack07/index.html)), then read most of whatever they choose, 77% online ([Brass Tacks Design](https://www.brasstacksdesign.com/eyetrack07.htm)). Game writers land in the same place: no more than two lines on screen, of 8 to 10 words each ([Angstadt](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-get-people-to-read-text-in-your-game)); 150 characters and "two sentences is all you ever need" ([Zynga's writers](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/social-players-don-t-want-to-read-your-text----or-do-they-)); "tweet length" ([Scott Benson](https://cartridgelit.com/2018/05/15/interview-scott-benson/)); and "if you give people at least half an excuse not to read something then they won't" ([Jon Ingold](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-to-remove-the-frustration-of-reading-in-games)). Each morning object therefore gets one or two sentences, and the picture or headline has to carry the callback alone, because the paragraph under it is optional.

Across about twenty games, news is read when it reports the player's own act in the world's voice, or when it hands over something usable. Hitman: Blood Money's paper sketches the player's face more accurately as notoriety grows ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitman:_Blood_Money)), and players liked "the newspaper recap … rather than showing a tabled list" ([Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198046980920/recommended/6860/)); No, I'm Not a Human's broadcast is watched because it gives the day's new sign ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No,_I'm_Not_a_Human)). News is skipped when it covers a world that goes on without the player, as in Not Tonight ("skipping entire text after the 20th day" ([Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198091396513/recommended/733790/))), when it is a thin feed, as in Death and Taxes ("boring, uneventful, and just simply detached" ([Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198003129733/recommended/1166290/))), when it ignores what the player did, as with That's Not My Neighbor's paper, which "does not get filled out when a resident gets killed" ([Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198056764604/recommended/3431040/)), and when it stands between the player and the decision ([Steam review of Suzerain](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198045162757/recommended/1207650/)). People carry consequences better than print. **Death and Taxes' boss, Fate, appears in 20.4% of positive reviews against 5.7% for its news feed, and Jorji in 4.2% against 1.2% for Papers, Please's paper** ([Steam review API](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/239030?json=1&language=english&num_per_page=0)).

The recommendation is to split the morning's three jobs across the objects that do each best, and to cut the words to about a third.

| Job | Carrier | Legible words | Why |
|---|---|---|---|

The front page keeps the masthead and puts the countdown in its date line. Under it go one photograph drawn by the game's portrait renderer, one headline and one caption line. The photograph is yesterday's decisive applicant: the registered unit's lit frame stamped REGISTERED, a human the clerk challenged, the clone, the Binnses. The headline logic stays, since the paper's `story()` already picks from the player's actual decisions, with pools such as "REGISTRY ADMITS A HUMAN WITH A WARRANTY" ([gen/gazette.ts](../src/gen/gazette.ts#L112); [gazette.ts](../src/content/gazette.ts#L10)). The Humanity Day special is already designed as the paper's last edition, with one photograph ([game-design.md](../notes/game-design.md)), so the daily paper becomes its set-up instead of a different format. On the morning of day 2, for instance, the photograph is the day-1 unit blinking and lit, stamped REGISTERED; the caption names it and the window; and the book lies open at Rule 2 with its slip, "In force from today, following yesterday's registration of a home robot at Window 3."

The rule then lives where it is used. The book opens each morning at the new page, whose pasted slip carries the rule and its cause, and over the week the slips build a deadpan Ministry changelog, the escalation Beholder's players quote rather than any single law ([Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198075061111/recommended/475550/)). The sticky note shrinks to a pointer ("Rule 2 is new."), the reminder of the current objective that accessibility guidance asks for ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)); today it restates the rule. The thread items leave print. Window 2's sign already reads VOTING and then FORKED, the posters already carry rule slogans ([hall.ts](../src/content/hall.ts#L65)), and the countdown can sit on a hall board. Likeness's release notes, which the robot arc needs because they announce the dimmer light, survive as the rulebook figure, which already draws each day's lamp at that day's size ([portraits.ts](../src/content/portraits.ts#L78)), plus at most one boxed line in the paper. The PA keeps the Ministry's voice but never carries a rule alone, since no essential information may depend on sound ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)); this game already learned that a crawling ticker read during the shift fails ([game-design.md](../notes/game-design.md)).

More original formats have good evidence but cost more. A morning post of one-line envelopes from people the clerk stamped would carry consequences through people, which the Fate and Jorji numbers favour, and the design already leaves Likeness's envelope on the desk after a paid unit; add it second, one line per envelope, only for specific acts. A wall of yesterday's registrations under status stamps would be native to a registry but needs art and screen room. Phone feeds, tickers and televisions that talk during play are the most criticised channels in the survey. One morning needs attention whatever the format: day 3 stacks the sign rule, the fork and Likeness's offer, and Pope kept heavy events off days with "an important bulletin message" because they consume "the player's headspace" ([Pope devlog, Nov 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-10/)). With the rule in the book, the letter competes less; if first sessions miss day-3 sign faults, the letter should move to day 2's evening statement. Replay argues for all of this, because seeded weeks and "Start day N again" repeat every line, and No, I'm Not a Human's replay complaints fall on fixed text: "all the radio and TV broadcasts stay the same" ([Steam review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198043969152/recommended/3180070/)).

## One pixel grid, object-owned fonts and a single violet

The owner's sense that the screens look generated has a documented basis. Anthropic's own frontend-design guidance lists "the commonest tells of a generated page", among them "Using all caps for labels", "Adding unnecessary typographic labels above content", "meta strings joined with middle dots", "a monospace face for small data labels", a warm cream background, "identical rounded cards" under "the same soft grey shadow", and a near-black background with a single bright accent ([Anthropic SKILL.md](https://github.com/anthropics/skills/blob/main/skills/frontend-design/SKILL.md)). The desk matches almost item for item: "FORM 1 · APPLICATION FOR REGISTRATION AS A HUMAN", "VIDEO SUBMISSION · PRINTOUT", "RULEBOOK · DAY 1" and "TIME · NO LIMIT" in tracked capitals, cream paper cards on soft shadows, and a black hall strip of orange monospace. The type is three system stacks, Courier New, Arial Narrow and Bradley Hand, with the Arial Narrow stack alone used in 61 places ([desk.css](../src/ui/desk.css#L20)), and Butterick calls system fonts "overexposed" because they ship on billions of computers ([Butterick](https://practicaltypography.com/system-fonts.html)). A fashionable replacement would not cure it. Anthropic's 2025 advice recommended monospace faces such as JetBrains Mono to escape the defaults ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/improving-frontend-design-through-skills)), and its 2026 guidance lists "a monospace face for small data labels" as a tell ([Anthropic SKILL.md](https://github.com/anthropics/skills/blob/main/skills/frontend-design/SKILL.md)); designers surveyed by Adrian Krebs name Space Grotesk, Instrument Serif and Geist as the language models' favourites ([Krebs](https://www.adriankrebs.ch/blog/design-slop/)).

The acclaimed desk games get their look from a rendering grammar, not a font count. Papers, Please runs at 570×320 pixels, scaled to "the highest possible whole-pixel scale" with black bars around it ([Pope devlog, Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)). It used **14, later 10, tiny pixel fonts** and still reads as one look, because every glyph sits on that grid ([Pope, Localizing Papers, Please](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/localizing-1/)), and Pope limited each object to "~3 shades" ([Pope devlog, Nov 2012](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-00/)). When he tried smooth vector text, the documents were "much harder to read" with "an inconsistent level of detail" ([Pope devlog, Mar 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-04/)), and he concluded: "Games that mix pixel resolutions make my eyes twitch" ([Apr 2013](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/tig-05/)). Pedro Medeiros's consistency rules say the same: one base resolution, nearest-neighbour scaling by one integer, a limited palette, and styles that "never leak from one world to another" ([Saint11](https://saint11.art/blog/consistency/)). Death and Taxes keeps greyscale plus three accents because emphasis "will immediately pop out" ([Placeholder Gameworks](https://placeholdergameworks.itch.io/death-and-taxes/devlog/102676/devlog-5-art)), and Unpacking held nearly 4,000 images to one style through its creative director's review ([Mucho Pixels](https://www.muchopixels.com/post/angus-doolan-interview)).

This game already has the grid where it matters most, in the hall and in the 40×48 portraits drawn at 2× with nearest-neighbour scaling ([hall.css](../src/ui/hall.css#L25)); the desk around them is smooth system text, which is Pope's "inconsistent level of detail" exactly. The stage also scales by a continuous, usually fractional factor ([Stage.tsx](../src/ui/Stage.tsx#L14)). MDN notes that `pixelated` scales to the nearest whole multiple and then smooths to the final size, and that when CSS pixels do not align with device pixels, some pixels are drawn larger than others ([MDN, image-rendering](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/image-rendering); [MDN, crisp pixel art](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Games/Techniques/Crisp_pixel_art_look)). The pixels most exposed to that are the lamp's 2×2 core and the day-7 slit, so **snapping the scale is a fairness fix as much as a style one**.

The recommended art pass draws the whole desk on the portraits' grid. With one art pixel equal to two stage pixels, the minimum stage becomes a canvas of 620×410 art pixels, close to Papers, Please's own. The stage scale snaps so that scale times the device-pixel ratio is a whole number, offsets round to device pixels, and the remainder is letterboxed, since "fractional physical pixels are not a thing" ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/device-pixel-content-box)). The stage's width already flexes and its height follows the scale, so most of that remainder becomes extra desk rather than bars, provided the layouts tolerate a desk taller than 820. Corners are drawn in pixels and shadows are hard, one art pixel in a palette colour, all lit from one direction. Stamps become pre-drawn rotated sprites instead of CSS rotations, and the blur filter goes. One master palette of roughly 24 to 32 colours, three or four shades per object, replaces the cream with the Ministry's own greys, paper stocks and stamp inks, passes a greyscale test, and reserves violet for the lamp alone, so that in a muted Ministry **the one saturated colour on screen is the robot's tell**. Motion answers the player's actions only, which even the anti-template guidance welcomes ([Anthropic SKILL.md](https://github.com/anthropics/skills/blob/main/skills/frontend-design/SKILL.md)).

Type belongs to objects. Three to five self-made pixel faces, each owned by a machine or a hand in the world, replace the system stacks: a Ministry print face with lowercase for the forms, the rulebook and the ledger; a dot-matrix face for the video printout and transcript; condensed stamp capitals; the supervisor's hand for the sticky note; and a double-size masthead for the paper. The repo already keeps a self-made 3×5 font as glyph grids in TypeScript and lists it as self-made art ([LICENSES.md](../public/assets/LICENSES.md)). A build script can turn grids like it into TrueType files with opentype.js, an MIT-licensed library that writes fonts from paths defined in code ([opentype.js](https://github.com/opentypejs/opentype.js)), so every face stays self-made, reproducible and served from the game's own files; the one-line justification for the new dev dependency is exactly that. Pope found no usable pixel-font editor and had to hack FontForge's tracer ([Pope, Localizing Papers, Please](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/localizing-1/)), a problem that generating from grids avoids. If a stopgap is wanted, m5x7, monogram, Public Pixel and Not Jam Mono Clean 8 are dedicated to the public domain on their own pages ([m5x7](https://managore.itch.io/m5x7); [monogram](https://datagoblin.itch.io/monogram); [Public Pixel](https://ggbot.itch.io/public-pixel-font); [Not Jam Mono Clean 8](https://not-jam.itch.io/not-jam-mono-clean-8)). Balatro's m6x11 asks for attribution ([m6x11](https://managore.itch.io/m6x11)) and most Google pixel fonts are OFL-licensed, so both fall outside the CC0-or-self-made rule, and any free itch.io font risks looking like one.

| Today | Replace with |

| "FORM 1 · APPLICATION FOR REGISTRATION AS A HUMAN" in tracked capitals | The form's own printed heading, part of its pixel artwork |
| "VIDEO SUBMISSION · PRINTOUT" | The tear-off header of the printer that made it |
| "RULEBOOK · DAY 1" | A spine and tabs on the book |

No source names key-cap chips as a tell; moving them onto objects is design judgement, in the spirit of Pope's pull chain and alligator clip on phones ([Pope mobile devlog](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/mobile/)). Readability sets floors the grid must respect. Xbox's guideline asks for **18 px of body height at 1080p on PC**, text that scales to 200%, sentence case for running text, and a non-stylised font option ([XAG 101](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/xbox/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/101)). A face with a 9-pixel body drawn at two device pixels per font pixel just meets that floor, while the 3×5 font at 2× is 10 px and should stay decoration. Text stays real DOM text in the pixel font rather than canvas drawing, so screen readers, selection and zoom keep working ([WCAG 1.4.5](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/images-of-text.html)), and a plain-font switch answers the call for a non-stylised option; a system font used only as that fallback is not a shipped asset, a reading of the licence rule the owner should confirm. CSS font-smoothing switches are non-standard and macOS-only ([MDN, font-smooth](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/font-smooth)), so crispness has to come from geometry, and a Playwright check at device-pixel ratios 1 and 2 that text regions contain only palette colours turns it into evidence. The expensive part is not the fonts, which three CSS variables re-skin at once, but snapping every position to the grid and re-measuring the layout budgets the e2e suite enforces, from the welcome letter, which already fails its hall-height check at 1240×820 with one sentence more, to the rulebook page heights ([rule0.spec.ts](../e2e/rule0.spec.ts#L87); [acceptance.md](../notes/acceptance.md)). That measuring is worth doing once, after the rule and paper changes have settled what the pages say, and pixel type will enforce the shorter texts the reading evidence already asks for.

## One sign-off, one batch of test edits, then a watched first session

The project's own rules set the order. AGENTS.md requires asking before changing gameplay, the rulebook or a module's public API, and forbids editing tests unless asked ([AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md)); the rule restructure does all four, so one owner decision should approve the design and the test edits together. The `human` rule id runs through the rule engine, the generator, the court, the paper's ordering, the save validator and the e2e helper, and a plain search finds **95 "Rule 0", "Memo 0" and "Fig. 0" strings in 27 files**, plus two escaped ones in the welcome letter ([judge.ts](../src/rules/judge.ts#L22); [save.ts](../src/ui/save.ts#L94); [gen/gazette.ts](../src/gen/gazette.ts#L108)). The lamp's own machinery, from the drawing and the lit frames to the figure, the jury tiers, the citation's film reprint and the Replaced ending's still, works with any rule id that reads it, so the art survives intact ([drawPortrait.ts](../src/gen/drawPortrait.ts#L521)). A single `face` id is cleaner than mapping `human` and `photo` onto one page, and since every Rule 0 string must be rewritten anyway, the cleaner route costs little more. Saves made before the change will no longer replay; the save system already sets such saves aside with a line that the Ministry has revised its forms ([game-design.md](../notes/game-design.md)).

Seeds decide how expensive that batch is. An in-memory probe of the generator found that the smallest version, the day-1 unit made valid and day 1's fake count cut to one, leaves the three exact-transcript pins (CHATTY, HOOMAN and SILENT) unchanged and days 2 to 7 identical over 600 seeds, except for **day-6 vouchers in 35 weeks (5.8%)**, because the unit leaves the list of refused names the generator draws from ([day.ts](../src/gen/day.ts#L354); [slice1.spec.ts](../e2e/slice1.spec.ts#L9)). It still breaks every test that pins day 1's make-up, the seed-127 week test and both replay snapshots ([slice2.spec.ts](../e2e/slice2.spec.ts#L53); [slice3.spec.ts](../e2e/slice3.spec.ts#L228); [replay.test.ts.snap](../src/__snapshots__/replay.test.ts.snap)). Anything that adds or reorders a random draw on day 1 moves every later day, and finding HOOMAN's transcript took **about 3.6 million seeds and 20 minutes on seven processes** ([project memory note](/Users/kemuru/.claude/projects/-Users-kemuru-repos-poh-papers-please/memory/e2e-seed-repick-cost.md)). That is why the day-1 phrase slip should be scripted without draws and checked with a days-1-to-6 hash against `main`, and why the restructure should land in one batch, paying the pin cost once.

The order that follows is the owner's decision first; then the rule restructure and the new morning together, since the rule notice moves from the paper into the book in the same change; then the art pass, measuring every layout budget once against final content. Each stage ends at the project's evidence bar of typecheck, unit tests, and e2e with screenshots. Then comes the step every acclaimed team relied on, from Valve's playtests ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2010/03/17/thinking-with-portals-making-a-test-chamber.aspx)) to the Golden Idol team watching each case "at least 5-7 times" in silence ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)): a recorded first session of days 1 to 3 by someone who has never seen the game, watched without help. It should answer five questions. Did they notice the day-1 light? Could they say what it meant the next morning? Did they read the caption, or go straight to the book? Did the face rule plus the clock stall them on day 2? And can they find the day-7 slit at real size and integer scale, with no hint?

## Conclusion

The four complaints share a root: the game tells the player things in text before they need them. The lamp's physics arrives in a letter before the first applicant, the day's rule in a paragraph of the paper, a label in capitals above every object. The fix is the same move each time: put each fact in the object where it is used, at the moment it is used, and let the player see before being told. The light belongs in a frame, the rule in the book, the consequence in a photograph, the heading in the form's own print. That discipline, more than any borrowed mechanic, is what Papers, Please's single grid and single rulebook add up to, and it makes the art pass and the rule pass closer to one project than they look: integer scaling protects the smallest robot tell, and pixel type enforces the word budgets the reading evidence asks for.

The restructure also makes the satire truer. The real registry states its humanity requirement in words, while the week as built gives the Ministry a check for the light from before the week began. A Ministry that registers the first robot lawfully and writes the check afterwards is the institution the game means to mock, learning late and in public, and it turns the tutorial day's second lesson into the week's first joke. The bet rests on two things nobody has yet watched: a new player noticing a light on day 1 that no rule reads, and the show-then-tell research, drawn from classrooms rather than games, holding at a registry window.
